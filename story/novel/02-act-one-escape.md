# THE UNFINISHED

## ACT ONE: ESCAPE

---

### Chapter 6: The Tollway

For three days nothing happened, and that was its own kind of frightening.

The river road ran north out of the valley and up onto the moor, and the moor went on. It was further into the Fade than Lowmarrow. The heather was a few scribbled tufts, the sky a wash of nothing, and the drystone walls on either side of the road were drawn as single lines with an occasional hatch to say *stone*. Sheep stood about in it like thumbprints. Every so often there was a farm, mostly outlines, with a thin figure in the yard and a thin god beside it, both of them raising a hand as Wren and Oren went by.

The red thread unwound behind them the whole way. Wren kept expecting the spool to be empty. It never seemed to get smaller.

Oren walked well. He walked the way he did everything now, with an easy, unbothered patience, and he never complained, and he never asked when they'd stop. When they did stop, at dusk, at whatever was there (a barn, a bridge, once a sheepfold with half a roof), he would unsling his lamplighter's pole without being asked, and find the nearest bracket or post or old lamp-hook, and light a lamp there. Then he'd sit down under it and wait for Wren to sit down too.

"Why do you do that?" Wren asked, the second night.

Oren looked up at the lamp he'd just lit on a fencepost, a small brave yellow in all that grey, as though he'd only just noticed it.

"I don't know," he said, interested. "Habit, I suppose. My hands do it." He turned them over and looked at them. "They know where the lamps go."

> ◆ **GAME** · **Lamps (rest points).** Oren lights them. Resting at one refills **Warmth** (healing charges), saves, and opens the **Letters** screen (from Chapter 8). Warmth is refilled by Oren's lamps *and* by people Wren has helped on the road, so later in the game an NPC who's been helped may refill a charge. The guardian comments on lamps on a cycle (*"He lights them because he doesn't know what else to do with his hands. You did that. And you warm yourself at it."*).

Wren slept badly. Wren always slept badly, but now it was worse, because Oren slept so well. He lay on his back under the lamp with his hands folded on his chest and slept like a stone in a river, all night, every night, without moving, and Wren lay awake and listened to him breathe and thought about a big scruffy orange dog that used to wake him four times a night to check the shutters.

On the fourth day the road went wrong.

It was a small thing at first. A milestone that said **TOLLWAY 6** and then, half a mile on, a milestone that said **TOLLWAY 9**. Then a stile in a wall that Wren was sure they'd climbed already. Then the road forked where it hadn't forked on the old map at the works, and both forks went uphill, and when Wren chose the left one it came down again, after an hour, at the same stile.

*Lost?* said the voice.

It hadn't spoken in three days. Wren had begun to hope, without letting themselves notice they were hoping, that four hundred miles was too far for it. That it really did live in Lowmarrow.

*I told you. I don't live in a place.*

There was a hill ahead that hadn't been there a moment ago. Wren watched it be drawn: a long curve of pencil, then shading, then a few strokes of heather, then a path up it that went nowhere. Behind them, the stile rubbed itself out.

"Oren," said Wren. "Do you see that?"

"Hm?" Oren looked about pleasantly. "It's a hill."

"It wasn't there."

"Wasn't it?" He considered the hill. "It's a nice hill."

Wren stood in the road with their heart going. Then, because there was nothing else to do, Wren did the thing they'd done in the square: shut their eyes, and felt for the warm frightened orange that had gone up their arm and into their chest four days ago, and was still there, curled up, waiting.

It woke. Wren felt it lift its head.

When Wren opened their eyes, the world had changed. Light came off Wren in a soft pulse, a ring of warm orange running out over the moor, and where it touched, things showed up: a sheep behind a wall, a hare in the heather, a scrap of rag on a fence. And the hill. The hill glowed faintly at its edges, a ghostly grey outline, like a sheet of tracing paper laid over the real world. Wren could see straight through it to the road behind, running on north, exactly where it had always been.

*That's cheating,* said the voice, offended.

> ◆ **GAME** · **WATCHLIGHT** (from the Lantern-Hound). Pulse that reveals hidden enemies and items through walls, briefly shows enemy attack telegraphs early in combat, and **makes the guardian's edits glow**. Any geometry the guardian has changed is outlined in grey. This teaches the exploration loop: the guardian rewires a route, Watchlight reveals the edit, and the player finds a way through or **follows the Red Thread back** to a known point.

Wren turned around. The red thread lay along the road behind them, bright as a cut, and it hadn't moved. The stile might be gone, the milestones might lie, but the thread ran straight back the way they'd actually come, through the hill as if the hill weren't there.

Wren followed it back a hundred yards, and found the real fork, and took the real road.

*Fine,* said the voice, after a while. *Fine. Have your thread.*

---

The Tollway was a long cut through the hills where four roads met, and it was nothing but inns. There had been tolls once, when there was trade. Now there were eleven inns along a single mile of road, mostly half-empty, competing for the carters and couriers and drovers who still came through. Each one was painted brighter than the last, trying to be seen. It was the most colour Wren had seen since Lowmarrow, and nearly all of it was signboards.

The Gallowgate Inn had a red door and a yellow sign and a fat, jolly landlord whose god was a fat, jolly tankard that sloshed. Wren paid for a room with the coins sewn into their coat, and Oren sat down by the fire and was asleep before his supper came.

The courier came in at nine.

She came in fast, the way people come in out of the rain, shaking the wet off a long oilskin coat, a satchel over each shoulder and another in her hand. She was talking before the door shut.

"— and *he* says, it's only a mile to Hexham, and I says, you said that at Morrow Cross, and he *was* right at Morrow Cross, I'll give him that, it *was* a mile, it just had a river in it. Evening, Tam. Evening, everyone. Room? Doesn't matter which. One night."

"It's always one night with you, Tess," said the landlord.

"It's always one night with everyone, Tam, some of us are just honest about it."

Her god came in behind her, folding itself to get through the door. It was tall and thin and made of letters: dozens of envelopes and folded notes and postcards, all stacked and creased into a long-legged figure like a heron made of post. It never quite came all the way in. It hovered half in the doorway, one long paper foot still out in the rain, as though it were about to go again at any moment.

She sat at Wren's table, because Wren's table was the one nearest the fire.

"Don't mind me," she said. "Tess Morrow. Courier. I'll be gone by six." She looked at Oren, asleep with his mouth slightly open, then at Wren. "That your dad?"

"Neighbour."

"Long way from home with a neighbour."

"He didn't feel strongly about staying."

Tess laughed. It was a good laugh, startled and loud. "I *like* that. I'm going to steal that. Where's home?"

"South."

"Everywhere's south of somewhere." She stretched her legs out to the fire. Her boots were worn right through at the heels. "Where you headed?"

"North."

"*Everywhere's* north of —" and she stopped, and narrowed her eyes at Wren, and grinned. "Oh, you're one of those."

"One of what?"

"Never stay for the second question." She pointed at Wren with her spoon. "I'd know. I'm one of those too. It's very annoying from the outside, I'm told."

> ◆ **GAME** · Hub conversation at the Gallowgate. This region has **the most banter in the game**. Tess and Wren are the same kind of person and recognize it immediately. Optional dialogue over the next two in-game days unlocks Tess's story and side errands around the Tollway, with the guardian taunting on the road between inns.

They talked until midnight. Wren hadn't talked to anyone until midnight in years. It was easy with Tess, the way it was easy with strangers, but it stayed easy past the second sentence, which was new. She talked about roads. She'd been everywhere, it seemed. She'd carried letters to Corran and Velle and the coast and the far north, into the Fade, to villages that were so faded the people were barely there, so thin you could see the wall through them. She'd been a courier eight years. She hadn't slept two nights running under one roof in six.

"Why?" Wren said.

"Why what?"

"Why one night."

Tess looked into the fire. Her god, in the doorway, rustled.

"There's a letter," she said, eventually, quite lightly, "in my bag. Bottom of the left one. It's been there two years."

"Who's it for?"

"Me."

Wren waited.

"From a friend," said Tess. "Nell. We were — well. She was my best friend, I suppose, since we were nine. She had a bad year. Really bad. The kind where her god wouldn't let her out of bed. And I was there for a bit. I was. And then I got a run to the coast, and I took it, and then I got another one, and I took that, and I just —" She made a little gesture with the spoon, a long line going away. "Didn't stop. Didn't go back. I wrote, at first. Then I stopped writing. Then about two years ago, *that* found me." She nodded at the bag. "Care of the Courier's Office, Corran. Her handwriting."

"What does it say?"

"No idea." Tess smiled at the fire. "Haven't opened it."

"Two years?"

"Two years. Carried it four thousand miles. Can't open it, can't burn it, can't send it back." She shrugged. "It's either *I'm better, where were you,* or it's *I'm not, where were you.* Or it's her mum writing to say she's not anything any more. And I don't want to know which. So it goes in the bag, and I go on the road. One night."

The god in the doorway had taken another step out into the rain. Only its head was inside now, a great folded envelope tilted toward the fire.

*She's like you,* said the voice, warmly, into Wren's ear. *She runs from everything that matters.*

Wren didn't answer.

*Let's see how fast.*

### Chapter 7: The Courier

Wren woke at four with the room the wrong shape.

The ceiling was too high. The window was on the wrong wall. Wren sat up in the narrow inn bed and watched the corner of the room draw itself further away: a pencil line extending, the wall going back and back, the floorboards adding themselves one at a time like a sentence that wouldn't end.

From down the corridor, a scream.

It was Tess. Wren knew it before they were out of bed. They went out into the corridor in their shirt, and the corridor was too long too: it went on, past Wren's door and Tess's door and fifty other doors that hadn't been there at bedtime, a long perspective down to a vanishing point. Every door was shut. Every door was banging. A wind was going through the inn that had no business being inside a building, and the wind was full of paper.

Letters. Hundreds of them. Envelopes and folded notes and postcards, whirling in the corridor like starlings, slapping against Wren's face and arms. Some of them were open, and Wren saw a line of handwriting on one as it went past, *— can't stop thinking about —*, and then it was gone.

At the end of the corridor, Tess was on her knees, with her hands over her head.

Her god was above her. It had come all the way in at last, and it had grown: it filled the corridor, a towering long-legged thing of folded paper, with envelopes for hands, and a great creased letter for a face, and it was *moving*. It kept moving. It took a step one way and then another, it opened a door and shut it, it started down the stairs and came back up. It looked like something trying to leave every room in the building at once.

Standing beside it, in the shadow at the end of the corridor, with one long pencil hand resting lightly on its back, was Wren's guardian.

*She was going to stay,* the guardian said. *Did you know that? She'd decided. You did that, with your sitting up till midnight. She was going to stay another night, and see the neighbour off in the morning, and maybe walk a bit of the way north with you. Can you imagine?*

It took its hand off the Courier's back.

*I just reminded her what happens to people who stay.*

The Courier turned and ran.

> ◆ **BOSS: THE COURIER** · An **avoidance** god. It **never attacks directly**. The fight is a **pursuit** across the three floors of the Gallowgate Inn, which the guardian keeps lengthening and rewiring. The Courier slams and **seals doors** behind it, collapses staircases, and drops parcels that unfold into **paper proxies** (small enemies) that fight for it. Chasing it directly fails: it always has one more door. The player has to use **Watchlight** to read its routes (its future path glows faintly as a trail of envelopes) and cut it off, herding it into a room with no exits. Cornering it ends the chase.

Wren went after it.

There was no deciding to. Tess was on the floor, and the thing that had put her there was Wren's, so Wren went. The Courier went through a door at the end of the corridor and slammed it, and the slam drew a line of pencil across the doorframe, sealing it like a page glued shut. Wren went through the next door instead, and found a staircase where a linen cupboard should have been, and went down it.

It was like chasing a thought you're trying not to have. Every time Wren got near, there was another door. The Courier went through the kitchen and out of the scullery and up the back stairs, folding itself through gaps, and every door it went through shut behind it with a sound like an envelope being licked. Small folded things dropped from it as it went, parcels that hit the floor and unfolded into little paper men with paper knives, and Wren fought them with a poker from the kitchen fire and kept going.

Wren stopped, on the second-floor landing, breathing hard, and shut their eyes, and woke the orange thing.

The pulse went out through the walls. And Wren saw it: not the Courier, but the way it was going to go. A faint trail of glowing envelopes, laid through the inn like a paper chase, through the attic and down the chimney-breast and along the eaves. The place it always ran to, if you let it.

There was one room in the Gallowgate with only one door. The landlord's strongroom, in the cellar, where the takings went. The trail went past it every time.

Wren got there first, and propped the strongroom door open, and then went and found the Courier, and didn't chase it. Wren walked toward it, steadily, and it went the only way that was left.

It went into the strongroom. Wren went in after it, and pulled the door shut, and stood with their back to it.

The Courier stopped.

It was crouched in the corner of the little stone room among the cashboxes, folded small, its long paper legs drawn up. It looked at Wren with its letter-face, and it didn't attack. It held out its envelope-hands, both of them, palms up, like someone who has finally been caught and is almost glad about it.

**[ TAKE IT ]**

The pencil prompt hung in the air between them.

And under it, for the first time, in much smaller letters, in Wren's own hand, so faint Wren nearly didn't see it:

**[ walk away ]**

> ◆ **GAME** · **The first moral choice.** The player can **Take It** or **walk away**. If they walk away: Wren turns, opens the strongroom door, and leaves the Courier in the corner. Three seconds later the guardian is in the doorway, its hand on the Courier's head, and the Courier comes back up **feral**, now fighting directly: fast, dangerous, paper-blades. Phase 2 of the fight, much harder. At the end of phase 2 the only prompt is **[ TAKE IT ]**. The game is honest: Wren cannot stop this yet. But it **remembers who tried**. That flag changes two lines later (Tess in Chapter 24 says *"You walked away, the first time. I remember that."*).

Wren turned around, and opened the door, and walked away.

They got three steps up the cellar stair before they heard it. A sound like a whole post office being thrown down a well. They turned back. The guardian was standing in the strongroom doorway, and the Courier was rising behind it, unfolded to its full height, and every one of its envelope-hands had a paper edge on it now, thin and sharp and shining.

*That was sweet,* said the guardian. *Did you think that was up to you?*

The Courier came out of the strongroom like a storm through a letterbox.

It took a long time. It was hard. It cut Wren across the forearm and across the cheek, thin paper-cuts that stung like wasps, and Wren hit it with the poker until the poker bent. And at the end of it the Courier was down on the cellar flags, its letters scattered all across the floor, its long legs folded under it, and the only words in the air were the guardian's.

**[ TAKE IT ]**

Wren took it.

Paper, this time. It ran up Wren's arm like the turning of pages, quick and light and restless, and it was in, and it was folded up small inside them somewhere next to the warm orange thing, and it was *still*. The Courier came apart into letters, and the letters lifted, and were gone.

The little gap at Wren's shoulder opened by a hair.

---

Tess was sitting on the inn's front step when Wren came out, with the dawn coming up grey over the Tollway. She had her satchels round her feet. She'd taken them all off. Wren had never seen her without a bag on her shoulder.

She looked up when Wren came and sat beside her. Her face was calm.

"Morning," she said.

"Tess. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. It was mine. The thing that did it. It was —"

"Mm," said Tess. She nodded. It was a nice, polite nod. "That's all right."

She reached down into the bottom of the left-hand satchel, and drew out the letter. It was soft with handling, grey at the corners, the address in a round, careful hand: *Miss T. Morrow, care of the Courier's Office, Corran.* She looked at it for a moment, turning it over.

Then she opened it. Just like that. She slid her thumb under the flap, and took out the single sheet, and unfolded it, and read it all the way through, in the cold morning light on the step, while Wren sat beside her and couldn't breathe.

Tess folded it up again.

"Huh," she said. "She wasn't well." She looked at the sheet again, as if checking a delivery address. "She asked me to come. She says I said once I'd always come." She thought about it. "That's a shame."

She put the letter down on the step beside her. She stood up, and stretched, and looked up and down the Tollway road, at all its painted signboards. She picked up one satchel, and then, after a moment's consideration, the other two.

"Well," said Tess. "I've got a run to Hexham. Safe travels."

She walked off down the road. She walked easily. She didn't look back. And she didn't hurry, Wren noticed; for the first time since Wren had met her, she was walking somewhere at an ordinary pace, like someone who might stop, if there were anything to stop for.

The letter was still on the step.

Oren came out of the inn behind Wren, yawning, rested, and saw it there, and stooped and picked it up. He looked at it with mild curiosity. Then he folded it once more and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

"Why did you do that?" said Wren.

Oren looked down at his coat, as though his hand had done it without consulting him.

"I don't know," he said. "Seemed a shame to leave it."

> ◆ **GAME** · **THRESHOLD** acquired (from the Courier). Combat: draw a door in the air and step through it (short teleport, invulnerable in transit). Exploration: seal or open certain passages, and take Courier shortcuts. **Oren now carries Tess's letter** (inventory, examinable: *"It isn't yours."*). It pays off in Chapter 24.

*You see?* said the voice, as they walked north out of the Tollway, with the red thread paying out behind them. *She's better. She's walking slowly. She's free.*

Wren didn't say anything.

*You did a kind thing. I don't know why you look like that.*

### Chapter 8: Letters

That night they stopped at a ruined chapel on the moor road. Oren found an old iron lamp-hook by the door and lit a lamp on it and sat down. Wren sat down too, on the step, and took out the paper and the pencil Mira had pushed into the bag on the night they left, and the stub of red sealing wax, and looked at them.

They'd tried to write twice already. Once at the sheepfold, and once at the Gallowgate the evening before it all went wrong. Both times the letter had come out the same.

> ◆ **GAME** · **LETTERS.** At any lamp, the player can write to Mira. The letter screen opens with most of the letter **already written by the guardian**, in pencil, waiting to be inked. The player picks one line per slot. Most options are the guardian's (variations on *fine*). Lower in the list, sometimes, there is one option in Wren's own ink: shorter, honest, harder to find. Mira's reply waits at the next waystation, and **answers whatever Wren actually sent**.

The first letter, from the sheepfold, had said:

> *Dear Mira,*
> *The road is fine. Oren is fine. I'm fine.*
> *Thank you for the thread.*
> *W.*

The guardian had written most of it, and Wren had let it. It was quick, and it was true in every fact, and it said nothing at all.

Mira's reply had been waiting at the Gallowgate, in the rack behind the bar, when Wren arrived. It was three pages long. It was all news. The blue vat had taken a little better on Tuesday and her father had stood in front of it and cried and then pretended it was the steam. Oren's house was empty and his cat was coming to the works for scraps and she'd started calling it Lamp, which she was aware was in poor taste. The Mender had mended the church bell, and now it rang flat, and the vicar was furious, and the Mender was very pleased with itself.

At the bottom, she'd written:

> *There's a cup on the windowsill.*
> *M.*

Wren had read it three times by the fire at the Gallowgate. Then put it away inside their coat, next to the spool.

*She's being kind,* the voice had said. *It's a letter. Letters are easy to be kind in. Wait until she has to look at you.*

Now, on the chapel step, Wren unfolded a fresh sheet. The letter was already there, in faint pencil, before Wren had touched it with the lead, waiting:

> *Dear Mira,*
> *The road is fine. Oren is fine.*
> *I'm ______.*

Wren looked at the blank.

*Fine,* the voice suggested. *Or "keeping busy." Or say sorry about the cup. She'd like that. It's sweet.*

Wren's pencil hovered. Below the blank, so faint it was barely there, so low on the page it was nearly off the bottom, there was another line. Not in pencil. In ink. Wren's own handwriting, cramped and small:

> *I took another one today. She opened her letter and felt nothing. I watched her do it.*

Wren stared at it. Wren hadn't written it.

Wren hadn't written it *yet*.

*Don't,* said the voice, quite gently. *What's she going to do with that? She's two hundred miles away with a dying works and a father who cries at vats. She's got enough to carry without you.*

Wren looked at the lamp. Oren was asleep under it with his hands folded on his chest. In his inside pocket, a letter he didn't know why he'd kept.

Wren crossed out *I'm ______.* Wren wrote the line at the bottom of the page. It took a long time, for one line. When it was done Wren folded the letter without reading it over, and sealed it with the red wax, and put it in the box at the next post-stage on the road north before there was time to think again.

---

The answer was waiting at Hexham, four days on.

It was short this time. One page. Wren opened it standing up in the post office, and then sat down on the bench by the door, because their knees had decided to.

> *Wren,*
>
> *I don't know what it's like to do what you're doing. I'm not going to pretend I do. I'd be lying, and you'd know.*
>
> *But you watched her. You could have looked away and you didn't. I don't think the thing behind your shoulder would have watched. I think it would have been very pleased with itself and gone to get a drink.*
>
> *Write anyway. Write when it's bad. Especially then. You don't have to say anything clever. You can just write "bad" and I'll know.*
>
> *Lamp the cat has had kittens in your old boots. I've left them there. You'll have to come and evict them yourself.*
>
> *There's a cup on the windowsill.*
>
> *M.*

Wren read it twice. The second time, they noticed their hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the middle of the first time.

*She doesn't know you,* said the voice. *She knows the person who stirs the blue vat and makes jokes about their face. Wait. Wait until she sees the rest.*

Wren folded the letter and put it inside their coat with the other one.

> ◆ **GAME** · If the player sent **only guardian lines**, the Hexham reply is the gentler, news-only variant, and the guardian line is *"See? She didn't even notice."* The honest variant (above) is canonical for the novel. Over Acts 1–2, Mira's letters respond to the **pattern** of what's been sent: careful and newsy if Wren is always "fine", direct and specific when Wren is honest. Mira never makes a speech in a letter. She sends news, and a question, and sometimes the cup.

### Chapter 9: Corran

Corran was on fire, and it was beautiful.

That was Wren's first thought, coming over the last hill and seeing it in the valley below: the whole town glowing. Then they understood. It wasn't fire. It was furnaces, dozens of them, glassblowers' furnaces in long brick sheds all down the river, every one of them burning, and every one of them lighting up the glass. And there was glass everywhere. The roofs of the town were glass. Great domed conservatories of it, and greenhouses, and the spire of the church was a single twisted column of green glass three hundred feet high, and the light from the furnaces came up through it all and lit it from underneath, gold and green and amber and deep blue.

Corran was the most vivid place Wren had ever seen. It was so thickly inked it was almost painful to look at.

"Oh," said Oren, beside them. He looked at it for a long time. "That's very nice," he said, and went on looking, with the face of a man trying to remember why that ought to matter.

---

Ambrose Pell's workshop was the largest on the river, and the quietest.

The furnace was lit. That was the first odd thing. The fire was going, roaring, white at the heart, and the long iron pipes were racked on the wall, and the marver bench was clean. But there was nobody at the bench. And the floor of the workshop, the entire floor, from wall to wall, was glass. Broken glass. Shards and splinters and great curved pieces of vessels, all colours, crunching underfoot, piled in drifts against the walls, heaped under the benches, and over it all a fine glittering dust that got into the throat.

Ambrose Pell was sitting on a stool in the middle of it all with a cup of tea, looking at the furnace.

He was older than Wren had expected, sixty perhaps, stout and grey-bearded, with forearms like hams, scarred white from the pipe. He looked round when Wren's boots crunched in the doorway, and his face did something tired and kind.

"Mind your feet," he said. "I'm afraid I'm not very tidy. Are you from the Guild?"

"No. Just — passing through. Someone at the inn said you were the best in Corran."

"Did they." Ambrose sipped his tea. "That was very kind of them. It was true, once, I suppose. I haven't finished anything in eleven years."

His god was behind him.

It stood beside the furnace, and it was the most beautiful god Wren had ever seen. It was armoured, in layers and plates of glass, every colour the furnaces made: shoulders of amber, a breastplate of deep green, gauntlets of a blue so clear you could see the fire through them. It was very tall and very still, and it held a polishing cloth in one gauntleted hand, and with it, slowly and endlessly, it was polishing its own shoulder.

There was a crack in the shoulder. A hairline. Wren could only just see it. The god polished it, and polished it, and the crack did not go away.

"The Ninth Draft," said Ambrose, following Wren's look. "That's what I call him. Because I always make it to the ninth draft. Eight are wrong, I can see they're wrong, I break them. The ninth's nearly right. I can see where it's nearly right. And just as I'm about to take it off the pipe, he says —" Ambrose stopped. "Well. He says it's not right. And he's not wrong. He's never wrong. And so I break it."

He gestured with his teacup at the floor, the glittering heaped floor.

"Eleven years of ninth drafts," he said.

*I like this one,* said the guardian.

Wren hadn't seen it come in. It was simply there, leaning in the corner of the workshop by the glory hole, its long pencil arms folded, looking at the Ninth Draft polishing its shoulder with an attention Wren had never seen it give anything.

*It understands. If you can't do a thing right, you shouldn't do it at all. That's all I've ever been trying to tell you.*

"I'm going north next month," Ambrose said. "To the Stillhouse. I've booked it. A Stilling." He looked at his god, which went on polishing. "I'm so tired, you see. I just want to make something. One thing. And I'll be honest with you, I don't know which of us will be making the work afterwards, him or me. Or neither. But I suppose —" he finished his tea, "— I suppose it doesn't matter, as long as something gets finished."

The guardian unfolded its arms.

It walked across the workshop, and the glass did not crunch under it. It stopped beside the Ninth Draft, and laid one long hand flat on its cracked amber shoulder, and the crack *spread*.

*There,* said the guardian, softly, to the god. *There. Now you can see it properly. Now you can see how wrong it all is.*

The Ninth Draft stopped polishing.

It turned its head. It looked at the floor of the workshop, at eleven years of broken glass, as if seeing it for the first time. And then it looked at Ambrose, sitting on his stool in the middle of it all. And it raised the polishing cloth, which was not a cloth any more but a long, curved blade of clear glass.

> ◆ **BOSS: THE NINTH DRAFT** · A **perfectionism** god. Its glass armour only breaks on **perfectly timed hits** (a frame-tight window, telegraphed by a glint). **Sloppy hits heal it**: a new layer of glass grows over the crack, visibly. But it spends long, beautiful phases **polishing itself**, standing still and lovingly correcting a single plate, and it is vulnerable then. It was the most beautiful thing Wren had ever fought, and the most patient. It never hurried. It came across the glittering floor in long, deliberate steps, and it cut in long, exact arcs, and every time Wren hit it, the glass healed, a new clean layer growing over the scratch like ice over a puddle. Wren hit it, and hit it, and every hit made it stronger.

*Do it properly,* said the guardian, from the corner, approvingly. *Or don't do it at all.*

Wren stopped hitting it.

Wren stood among the shards with the bent poker in their hand and watched it. And it stopped, too. Halfway through a stroke. It lowered its glass blade and stood in the furnace light, and turned its head, and began, slowly, lovingly, to polish the plate on its left forearm, where Wren's last hit had scratched it. It forgot Wren was there. There was only the scratch, and the cloth, and making it right.

Wren waited for the glint. And when the light from the furnace caught the crack in the shoulder, just so, Wren struck once.

The amber shoulder broke.

It went on like that. It took a long time. Wren learned to stand still on the broken glass and watch, and wait, and strike only when they were sure, and when they weren't sure, not to strike. The Ninth Draft re-forged itself, plate after plate, faster and faster, the glass growing back across it in sheets, and Wren broke the plates before they set, and broke them, and broke them. And at last it knelt, among eleven years of broken drafts, with its armour in pieces around it and nothing underneath but a shape of light.

**[ TAKE IT ]**

Wren took it. It went into them cold and sharp and clear, like a mouthful of ice water, and then it was still.

Ambrose had not moved from his stool through any of it. He sat there, with his empty teacup in his hands, and his face was smooth.

"Well," he said, after a while. He looked around the workshop with mild interest. "That's very quiet."

They stayed the night in Corran, at an inn with a glass roof, and Wren lay on their back and watched the furnace light play over the glass all night and didn't sleep.

In the morning, on their way out of town, they passed Ambrose's workshop. The door was open. The furnace was going.

Ambrose was at the bench, with a long iron pipe in his hands, turning it. On the end of the pipe there was a bowl. A perfect bowl. Thin as a bubble, the colour of green water, without a flaw in it anywhere. He'd made it in a night.

He saw Wren in the doorway and held it up, pleased.

"Look," he said.

Wren looked. It was perfect. There was nothing wrong with it at all. And it was the most lifeless thing Wren had ever seen; it looked like a picture of a bowl.

"It's lovely," said Wren.

"Is it?" Ambrose turned it in the light. "I suppose it must be." He set it down on the bench, and picked up another gather of glass on the pipe, and began on the next one.

### Chapter 10: The White Road

North of Corran, the world ran out of ink.

It happened gradually, over five days. The hedges went first: they turned from drawn hedges into a single line along each side of the road with a few scribbles for leaves, and then into a line with no scribbles, and then into nothing, just a slight greyness where a hedge would be. The road itself got paler. The sky had stopped being any colour at all. By the third day it was hard to tell where the ground stopped and the sky began. The horizon was an idea. Wren and Oren walked on a white page with a faint grey path drawn across it, and the red thread lay behind them on the white like a vein.

There were villages. They were the worst of it.

They were whole. They were neat. The houses were drawn in fine, clean, very faint lines: a door, two windows, a chimney, a little curl of smoke. And the people in the villages were drawn the same way, faint and clean, and they were working. Hanging washing. Mending fences. Sweeping steps. They smiled when Wren went by. They said good morning. Their gods walked beside them, small and grey and silent, and none of the gods was anything in particular: they were the size of cats or dogs, and grey, and they followed their hosts at a polite distance, and that was all.

"Is it always like this?" Wren asked a woman at a well, who was drawing water with a slow, pleasant, unhurried motion. "Up here?"

"Like what, love?"

Wren didn't know how to say it. "Quiet."

The woman thought about it, smiling, and nodded. "Oh, yes. We all went, you see. To the Stillhouse. The whole village. Years ago, now. Before my time as reeve." She hauled up the bucket. "It's ever so peaceful."

Her god sat on the well-head. It might once have been a bird. It was hard to tell any more.

*Look at them,* said the voice. And Wren was surprised to hear something in it they had never heard before, something almost like longing. *Nobody up here has ever had a bad morning. Not one. Not one in thirty years. Can you imagine?*

Wren could.

Oren, beside them, looked at the village, and the well, and the woman with her pleasant face, and he nodded slowly. He looked, Wren thought, more at home than he had looked anywhere since Lowmarrow.

"It's nice here," Oren said.

> ◆ **GAME** · **The White Road.** A long, quiet traversal through the deep Fade. Very few enemies. The world is almost entirely white with faint grey linework, and the **Red Thread** is the only saturated colour on screen. Stilled villages are explorable. NPC dialogue is uniformly pleasant and uniformly empty, and the player can't get a single NPC here to say anything with feeling. The guardian is unusually quiet, and its few lines here are *wistful*. **Visual note:** by now the gap in Wren's outline runs from the left shoulder to the elbow. It isn't called out, but a player who looks will see it, and the camera's resting position starts favouring Wren's right side.

On the fifth day the Stillhouse came up over the white.

It was enormous and it was perfect. That was the only word. It stood on a white hill, and it was a white citadel, all towers and arches and long colonnades, and its every line was clean. Not a smudge. Not a hatch out of place. It looked like an architect's drawing of an ideal building, made with a ruler and a fresh nib by someone who had never in their life made a mistake. The longer Wren looked at it, the less it looked real.

Wren had been here before. At fifteen and sixteen. Two years in a white cell in the east tower, learning to fold their hands. Wren had forgotten, until now, how it made you feel to look at it: like a smudge on a clean page.

There was someone waiting at the gate.

She was standing quite alone, in the white, in a plain white robe with a white shawl over her shoulders. She was older than Wren remembered, past sixty now, grey-haired and narrow and upright. She was drawn in the cleanest lines Wren had ever seen on a person. They were perfect and closed and unshaded, like a saint in a window. There was no god beside her. There never had been, in Wren's memory.

Mother Severine Voss, Stillkeeper of the Order of the Still Hand, came down the last few steps of the white road and took both of Wren's hands in hers. Her hands were warm.

"Wren," she said. She smiled, and it went all the way up into her eyes. "I hoped you'd come back. I've thought about you so often."

Wren couldn't speak.

"You were the most interesting student I ever failed," said Severine. And she said it with such simple, uncomplicated kindness that Wren, to their own astonishment, began to cry.

Severine didn't seem surprised. She put an arm around Wren's shoulders, and turned, and walked them in through the white gate, out of the white road, into the Stillhouse. And the red thread followed them in across the clean flagstones of the courtyard, the only colour for a hundred miles.

Behind them, Oren stood in the gateway, looking up at the white towers with his pleasant, peaceful face.

"Oh," he said, to no one. "This is nice."
