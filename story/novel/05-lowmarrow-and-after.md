### Chapter 25: Hollin's Lamp

The last lamp before Lowmarrow was an old lamp-house on a hill above the river road. It was a little round stone tower where, a hundred years ago, a keeper had lived to tend the beacon that guided the carts down off the moor. The roof was half gone. The beacon was a rusted cage. From the doorway you could see down into the valley, and the town at the bottom of it, grey on grey, the lamp in the square a black pin in the middle, and a few windows lit.

Two days' walk. One, if Wren could still walk properly, which they couldn't.

Oren lit the beacon. He went up the inside stair with his pole, and fiddled in the rusted cage, and found something that would burn, and lit it, and came down. Then he lay down on his back by the hearth with his hands folded on his chest and went to sleep.

Mira sat by Wren in the doorway. The Mender sat behind her. There was no more thread. It had the hem of her coat in its fingers, what was left of it, and it was already unpicking.

> ◆ **GAME** · **Hollin's Lamp.** Final rest point before Lowmarrow. When the player rests, the Mender begins its stitching animation. The scene does not play out automatically. **The player must act.**

Wren put a hand over the Mender's.

Its long woollen fingers stopped. The needle stopped. The Mender looked at Wren, with no face to look with.

"Stop," said Wren. "Please."

"It's nearly holding," said Mira.

"It's not. It held for an hour today." Wren said it gently. "An hour, Mira. Less. It went at the stile." Wren looked at her coat, which came to her knees now and had come to her ankles at the Stillhouse gate. "You can't sew me shut. You've nothing left to do it with."

Mira's face did something terrible. It crumpled, and she fought it, and it crumpled anyway, and she was furious and crying both at once.

"Then what am I supposed to *do?*" she said. "I walked three hundred miles, Wren. I followed a *thread*. I'm not — you don't get to just — I'm supposed to *do* something."

"I know."

"So what do I *do?*"

"I don't know." Wren looked down at the valley, at the town, at the dark pin of the lamp. "Can I tell you what it says?"

Mira went very still.

"You've seen it," Wren said. "Your whole life. Behind me. Everyone in Lowmarrow's seen it, looking over my shoulder, the way people do. But you can't hear it. Nobody can hear it. You never once asked what it says." Wren took a breath. "I've never told anyone. Not in fifteen years. It made very sure of that. I'd like to tell you."

Mira sat down. Properly, on the stone step, beside Wren, facing them.

"All right," she said.

"It says nobody will notice if I don't get up," Wren said.

It came out strange. Wren had heard it so many times, in that other voice, older and flatter, from just behind the shoulder. Wren had never heard it in their own voice before, out loud, in the cold air. It sounded smaller. It didn't sound less true.

Mira didn't answer straight away. When she did, she said it quite steadily.

"I was going to come and knock," she said. "That first morning. I had my coat on." She looked at Wren. "Every morning, Wren. I had my coat on every single morning for two years. I used to stand at the gate with my coat on and give you five more minutes. And then five more. And you always came. You always came round the corner of Mill Street just before I went to get you."

Wren hadn't known that.

"It says you only ask because you're kind," Wren said. "Whether it's bad."

"I'm not that kind," said Mira. "Ask anyone at the works."

"It said you'd cut the thread. Inside a month."

"I didn't cut it." She looked at the Mender's empty hands. "I ran out. That's not the same thing."

"It said letters are easy to be kind in. It said wait until you have to look at me."

"I'm looking at you," said Mira.

She was. She hadn't looked away once.

"It says I hurt everyone who stands near me," said Wren. "It said I couldn't even get out of a line without hurting someone."

Mira thought about Oren, asleep by the hearth with his hands folded. She didn't pretend not to.

"Some of them," she said. "Not the way it means."

"It says you'll leave," said Wren. "When it gets bad. When you see."

Mira didn't say anything to that one for a while. She looked down at the valley, and the town, and back at Wren. At the paper side of Wren, the coat hanging off it. At the frayed stitches.

"Is this not bad?" she said, very quietly.

Wren went on. It took a long time. There were more of them than Wren had thought: fifteen years of them, stacked up, a whole ledger. *You're very funny with strangers. You know why? Because they leave.* "I'm not a stranger." *He follows you because he has nowhere else to be.* "He follows you because his hands know where the lamps go. You're one of the lamps." *You'll make her watch.* Mira didn't answer that one. She only nodded, because it was true, and she was going to watch, and she wasn't going anywhere.

They went on, and Mira answered every one. Some of them she couldn't answer, and said so.

At the edge of the firelight, in the corner of the lamp-house, the guardian sat listening.

It had come up out of the floor while Wren was talking. Wren had seen it come, out of the corner of an eye, and hadn't stopped. It sat with its knees drawn up in Wren's coat, the way Wren sat, and the face it had been making since Hexham was nearly done now. The eyes were in. The mouth. It was listening to its own words, fifteen years of them, said out loud in Wren's voice to someone else, and listening to someone answer them.

It didn't get any smaller. Wren had half thought it would. It only sat, and listened, and was very calm.

There was one left.

"It says we won't do that again," said Wren.

"Do what?"

"This." Wren looked at her. "Telling."

And Wren told her the rest. The kitchen light on in the middle of the night. The tap running. The heron and the plate. The poster with the smiling sun. A child standing in a line at the town hall with no breakfast, and an old woman leaning over a desk to see them, and two Stillers at the door at three o'clock, and Mam looking at Wren in the passage before she went to get her coat. The quiet through the wall that night. How glad Wren had been. How the bird on the pillow had stopped being a bird.

"I sent her," said Wren. "I'm the reason she's like that. And I was glad."

Mira was crying. She didn't wipe it. She didn't let go of Wren's hand to do it.

"You were nine," she said.

"I know."

"You were *nine*, Wren. Your mam was on the kitchen floor every night and you were nine and you went and told someone. That's what you're supposed to do." Her voice broke on it and she kept going. "That's what you're *supposed* to do. It was them. They answered it with *that*. It wasn't you. It was what they had to give."

Wren didn't say anything. Wren couldn't. They sat in the doorway with their good hand in Mira's, and the thing they'd carried since nine sat between them in the cold, out loud.

In the corner, the guardian spoke for the first time that night.

*There,* it said. Not to Mira. She couldn't hear it. To Wren. Mild, and patient, and certain. *Now she has to carry it too.*

---

Mira took the Mender's needle-hand in hers.

The Mender let her. Together, slowly, she and her god began to unpick the last row of stitches down Wren's arm. One at a time. The thread came out of the edges of Wren's line, and the gap opened under it, paper-white, edged with faint other people's colours, orange and the dim held-breath grey of the Veiled. Mira looked at it the whole time. She didn't look away.

When the last stitch was out, she wound the bit of thread round her fingers and put it in her pocket.

"All right," she said.

"All right?"

"All right." She looked at the gap. "Then I'll just be here."

"That's not much," said Wren.

"It's what I've got."

She kissed Wren.

It was brief, and clumsy, and her nose was cold, and Wren's mouth missed slightly at first. It was the way it is between two people who've waited two years in a works yard with a cup between them, each certain the other one never would, and who have found out too late to have much time to be wrong about it.

The guardian watched. It didn't look away. It watched the way you'd watch someone else's life through a lit window from the street.

---

They slept side by side on the floor of the lamp-house, under the beacon, in their coats. Wren didn't sleep well. Neither did Mira. Wren could hear her not sleeping. They lay there in the dark, both awake, listening to Oren breathe and the beacon tick and the wind on the moor.

It always had. Wren lay on the stone floor in the grey light and did it in parts: eyes, then hand, then the edge of the coat. It took exactly as long as it always took.

When Wren was up, sitting, with their back to the wall, a mark drew itself on the stone beside their shoulder.

Wren saw it happen. For the first time in fifteen years Wren actually *saw* one happen. The guardian was sitting beside them, against the same wall, and its hand moved. A thin grey stroke, upright, drawn very carefully from the bottom to the top, the way a clerk enters a sum in a column he doesn't like the look of. One. And then its hand went back into its lap.

"What are those?" said Wren.

The guardian didn't answer. It looked at the mark for a while, the way you'd look at a bill.

Beside Wren's bedroll there was a cup.

Mira was at the hearth with a tin kettle, not looking round. Milk, no sugar. It was still warm.

Wren drank it before it went cold.

### Chapter 26: Lowmarrow

They came down into Lowmarrow in the late afternoon, with snow coming.

The town was deep in the Fade now. Wren saw that as soon as they came off the moor road onto the bridge. It had been thin when they left; now it was barely there. The cobbles were a few faint lines. The shopfronts had lost their lettering. The houses were pale outlines with a door and two windows, like the houses on the white road. The baker's awning wasn't any colour at all. The people in the streets were faint, and slow, and the gods walking beside them were small and grey.

> ◆ **GAME** · **Lowmarrow, revisited.** The prologue map, deep in the Fade. The underdrawing around Wren is now only a few yards across. Free roam is short. Key interactions: **the dye works gate** (the knot is still on the post), **Wren's mother's doorstep**, **Wren's room** (the tally wall, the box), and **the square**.

The knot was still on the dye works gatepost. Neat and tight, the way the Mender had tied it. The thread was long gone from it. But the knot was there. Wren put a hand on it as they went past.

Mira's father came out of the counting-house as they came through the yard. He was an old man now. He hadn't been, eight months ago. He looked at Mira, at her coat cut down to nothing and her boots tied together with string, and he looked at Wren, and he didn't say anything about either of them. He held his daughter for a long time in the snow.

"The blue's gone grey," he said, into her hair. "All of it. I don't care. You're home."

---

Wren's mother was on her doorstep at the top of Cobble Lane, shelling nothing. There were no peas in winter. She sat on the step with an empty bowl in her lap, and her folded grey god beside her, and watched the snow.

"Hello, love," said Ada Tolley. "You look tired."

"I am tired."

"It's a tired sort of face," she agreed pleasantly. Then she looked at Wren more carefully, with the faint interest that was the most she had. "You're thinner, love. On the one side."

"Yes."

"Mm." She looked at the snow. "I've missed you on Sundays."

She said it the way she'd say it looked like rain. Wren sat down on the step beside her anyway, in the snow, with their paper side against the doorframe. Mira waited at the bottom of the lane.

"Mam," said Wren.

"Yes, love?"

Wren opened their mouth.

*We won't do that again,* said the guardian.

It was standing in the lane in front of the step, in Wren's coat, with Wren's nearly finished face, and it said it quite gently, the way you'd stop a child from touching a stove. *Look at her. Look what telling did to her the first time. You want to do it again? To her face?*

Ada was looking at the guardian. Anyone could see a god. She looked at it with mild interest, and then at Wren, and then back at it.

"That's a funny thing," she said. "It's got your coat on."

"Mam, I —"

Wren couldn't. It wasn't that the words weren't there. They were all there, in order, from the kitchen light to the coat on the hook. They simply wouldn't go past the guardian. Wren sat on the step with their mouth open and nothing came out, the way nothing had come out of their mouth in fifteen years of dialogue wheels with no honest line on them.

"Never mind," said Wren.

"All right, love."

Wren got up. Ada watched them go down the lane with her empty bowl in her lap.

"That'll be nice," she said, to nobody, about nothing.

---

The room hadn't changed. Somebody, Mira probably, had been in and dusted. The clock had stopped. The box was still under the bed.

And the wall was still covered in marks.

Wren stood in the doorway and looked at it. Fifteen years of them. Thousands. Faint pencil strokes in their little fenced groups of five, from the skirting board up as high as a person could reach lying down, and under the bed, and along the wall behind the wardrobe. Wren had seen one made now. They knew whose hand made them. They still didn't know what they were for.

Wren knelt, slowly, because the left leg barely bent any more, and pulled the box out from under the bed with their good hand.

The white sash. And under it, the copybook.

Wren had not opened it since they were nine. They opened it now. Birds, on every page, in a child's careful pencil. Wrens. Robins. A blackbird with a worm. A heron that was very obviously Mam's. Page after page.

And then the last drawing.

A kitchen. A woman at a sink with her back turned. A tall grey heron, and a broken plate drawn very carefully in pieces on the floor. In the corner, on the stairs, small, a child with a round brown bird on their shoulder. Underneath, in a nine-year-old's best handwriting:

*Mam is sad. Tomorrow I will tell the town hall.*

Every page after it was blank.

Wren sat on the floor with it open on their knee for a long time. Behind them, in the doorway, the guardian stood and waited, and didn't say anything, because there was nothing it needed to say. It had been right there on the stairs.

Wren closed the copybook and put it in their coat.

### Chapter 27: Cobble Lane

Wren went back up the lane at dark.

Mira came as far as the bottom of it. She didn't ask what for. She had seen the copybook. Wren had shown her in the square, on the bench by the lamp, with the Hound growling the whole time at the thing standing three yards off in Wren's coat.

"I'll be here," she said.

Wren started up the lane.

The guardian was waiting halfway.

It was standing in the middle of Cobble Lane in the snow. It wasn't Wren's height any more. It was going up as Wren watched, a line at a time, the way it had gone up in the town hall on the first day: twice Wren's height, long-armed, folding together out of itself. But it wasn't the faint grey underdrawing of the town hall. Every line was ink now. Heavy and dark and certain. Wren's coat hung on it like a flag on a pole. Wren's face was on it, nearly finished, very calm, looking down.

*We won't do that again,* it said.

"We will," said Wren.

*You couldn't, this afternoon.*

"I know."

*Then what's different?*

Wren didn't have an answer. Nothing was different. Wren was more tired than they had been this afternoon, and had less of an arm, and had given away the last warm thing they had. Wren started walking anyway.

The guardian put out one long inked hand, and Cobble Lane changed.

It was the hardest fight of Wren's life.

The lane went long. Ada's door went away up a perspective of ropes and faces until it was a dot. The snow went to grid. And the queue was there, the whole of Lowmarrow, standing between the ropes in their pale coats with pencil circles round their heads and crosshairs where their eyes should be, every face turned toward Wren. Mrs Penhallow. The Abney boys. Bram Hollis with his grey terrier. Mira's father. All of them watching, the way the town hall had watched, the way the square had watched with the gridded cobbles.

*Look at them all,* said the guardian. *Look at them looking.*

It came down the lane like a weight of water, the Undertow's slam drawn in ink, and Wren went sideways the way the Hound had taught them, and it was already there.

*You go sideways. You always go sideways. I've watched you go sideways for fifteen years.*

It drew a door in the air and came through behind Wren. It struck with the Draft's glint on its hand and Wren felt the stitches go, all of the last of them, in one long rip down the side. It was wearing Wren's face while it did it.

*You told her,* it said. *At the lamp-house. Did you see her face? You did that. Everyone you tell has to carry it after. That's all telling is. Handing it to someone else.*

Wren went Unwatched.

The world went quiet and soft at the edges, and the guardian stopped. It turned its head this way and that, the way the Hound turned for foxes. Wren was invisible, and the lane was easy, and Wren walked up it among the ropes with the guardian blind behind them. It was so easy. It was the easiest thing Wren had done in months.

The doorstep at the top of the lane was gone.

Not gone: unseen. Wren knew how the Veiled worked by now. There were things that only existed when nobody was looking, and there were things that only existed when somebody was, and a door you knocked on to say something to someone's face was the second kind.

Wren stood in the snow, invisible, with the guardian searching behind them and nothing at the top of the lane.

Then Wren let go of it.

The guardian found them at once. It came at them out of the grid with everything it had, every trick, every habit, every god's power drawn in ink, and Wren didn't run. Wren couldn't run any more. Wren walked. Up the lane, between the ropes, under the eyes. It hit them, and they went down, and they got up. It hit them again. Wren couldn't have said afterwards how many times. Enough. The lane was red with the last of the Mender's thread, coming off them in rags. The guardian struck and struck. And somewhere in the middle of it, Wren stopped trying to hit it back and only walked, with it on them like weather, until there was a doorstep under their feet, and a door, and Wren put their hand flat on it.

Behind them, the guardian stopped.

It was Cobble Lane in the snow, at night, empty, with a lamp lit at the bottom of it where Mira was standing.

Wren knocked.

---

Ada opened the door in her nightgown, with a candle. The heron was folded on the mat behind her.

"Wren, love," she said. "It's late."

"Can I sit down, Mam?"

They sat on the step. Ada brought a blanket out for her knees, and one for Wren's, and sat with the candle between them, pleasant and patient.

Wren told her.

It came out plain. The kitchen light. The tap. The heron and the plate. The poster. The line at the town hall, and the old woman leaning over the desk, and *can you help her*. The two of them at the door at three o'clock. Coming home to the quiet.

"It was me," said Wren. "I sent them. You didn't go on your own. I asked them to come."

Ada thought about it. It was a long way back. The Stilled could remember. It was only that remembering didn't seem to matter.

"Oh, yes," she said. "I remember. They said a little one had come in on their own." She smiled at the candle. "That was good of you, love."

"I was glad," said Wren. "After. When it went quiet. I lay in bed and I was glad. I've been —" Wren's voice went entirely. It came back. "I've been sorry for fifteen years, Mam. I've been sorry every single day."

Ada looked at them with mild, kind interest, the way she'd look at a neighbour's new hat.

"Well," she said. "I don't mind, love."

She didn't. Wren could see that she didn't. There was nothing in her that could. Whatever the woman at the sink would have said, with her back turned and the tap running, the woman at the sink was not here to say it. She had been gone since Wren was nine, and Wren had sent her, and now there was nobody left in the world who could forgive them for it.

The heron on the mat did not move.

Wren sat on the step with the blanket over their knees.

After a while Mira came up the lane. She'd seen the lamp and the candle and the two of them on the step, and she'd waited as long as she could. She sat on the step below them, and put her hand on Wren's paper knee, and didn't say anything.

"Is this your young lady?" said Ada.

"Yes," said Wren.

"That's nice," said Ada Tolley.

At the bottom of the lane, under the lamp, the guardian had come up out of the cobbles again. It wasn't big. It was Wren's height. It stood in the lamplight in Wren's coat, and as Wren watched, the last lines went into its face, and it was finished.

It was Wren. A clean, inked, perfect drawing of Wren. The same face, with everything in its place. Only calmer. Rested. It looked the way Wren would have looked if Wren had ever once slept through a night. It looked, Wren thought, exactly the way Ada looked.

It didn't say anything. It didn't need to. It stood under the lamp and waited, with all the patience in the world, for Wren to come back down.

### Chapter 28: Mornings

> ◆ **GAME** · **Playable days.** The player now lives **a little over a week** of ordinary Lowmarrow days as Wren. Each begins with **[ GET UP ]**. Each day the hold takes **slightly longer**: eleven seconds, then twelve, then fourteen, seventeen, twenty, twenty-four. The game never comments on it. Each morning a tally mark is drawn when Wren is up, and the camera can see the guardian draw it. During the day the **dialogue wheel now offers Wren's own ink** alongside the guardian's pencil, and the honest lines are easy to find. The days are quiet: the works, the cup, Mira, Oren's fire, Sunday with Ada. Some are bad. Some are almost good. Nothing is resolved.

The first morning it took eleven seconds. Wren lay facing the wall and did it in parts, and got up, and the guardian drew a mark. It drew it the way it had at the lamp-house, carefully, like a clerk.

Wren went to work.

They walked down Cobble Lane and past the doorstep, where Ada was sitting in the cold with her bowl, and said good morning, and Ada said good morning. They walked across the square. Mrs Penhallow was opening the bakery, and her speckled hen was counting the loaves.

"Wren, love," she said. "You're back. You look —" She stopped. She looked at the left side of Wren, the coat hanging off nothing. "Well, you look," she said, and didn't finish.

"I know," said Wren. "It's bad. It's all right. How's the hen?"

And they stayed for the second sentence. Mrs Penhallow told them about the hen, which had started counting the loaves twice, and about her knees, and about the flour, which wasn't what it was. Wren listened. It wasn't easy and it didn't feel like anything special. It was only standing still in front of someone for longer than they had in fifteen years.

Mira was at the gate with the cup.

Wren drank it.

---

The works was the same. Three vats lit, eleven hands. Wren couldn't lift the paddle any more with one arm, so Callow put them on the line, hanging the bolts, which could be done one-handed if you were slow. The blue came out of the vat and dried two shades paler on the line. Callow came through at eleven and looked at it and said, "Well," and went out. Nothing about the Fade had changed. Wren hadn't thought it would. Four gods gone home was four people. The world was a great deal bigger than four people.

At noon a letter came from the Stillhouse. Severine's handwriting was small and very straight.

> *Wren,*
>
> *The grid has gone from the south road. The waystation is inked again, and the family remember nothing of it. Corran's watcher reports that the glassblower Pell has broken everything he has made since the summer and is the happiest wretch in the town. I do not understand what you have done. I would like to. If you are able, come north in the spring and tell me.*
>
> *I hope it is not too bad.*
>
> *S. V.*

Wren read it twice and put it in their coat with the copybook.

*Spring,* said the guardian, beside them on the line, pegging nothing.

---

The second morning took twelve seconds. The third took fourteen.

On the third evening they went to Oren's. He had a fire, as he'd said, and the Hound was up half the night, as he'd said, and it would not settle while the guardian was in the room. It lay across the doorway with its lantern-heart burning and its eyes on the thing in Wren's coat sitting quietly by the hearth, and every so often it growled, low, and Oren put a hand on it and didn't tell it to stop.

"He's been like that every night," said Oren. "Since you came home. He gets me up to look at your window."

"Oren."

"I know. I know. Four times a night. I go to the end of my yard and I look up Cobble Lane at your window, and the light's off, and I go back to bed." He rubbed his face. "I'd forgotten what it's like. Having to look."

Wren didn't know what to say to that. They said, "Thank you," and he said, "Don't thank me. It's awful," and they sat by the fire, the three of them and the Hound and the Mender and the guardian, and nobody said much, and it was nearly a good evening.

---

On the Sunday Wren went to their mother's for dinner, as they'd said they would at the beginning of all this. Ada made a stew. It was a good stew. She asked if Wren had had a nice week, and Wren said, "No, Mam. Not really," and she said, "Oh, that's a shame," and passed the bread.

---

The fifth morning took twenty seconds. On the fifth morning Wren lay facing the wall for a long time before the hand found the edge of the blanket, and the guardian sat on the end of the bed, very still, very patient, in Wren's coat, with Wren's rested face, and didn't say anything at all.

It didn't say anything most mornings now. It didn't have to. Wren knew what it would have said. It had said it for fifteen years. Hearing it once more wouldn't have changed anything, and the guardian had always known the difference between what needed saying and what didn't.

When Wren was up, it drew the mark.

---

The sixth day was almost good. The seventh was bad.

On the evening of the seventh, at the works gate, with the lamps coming on down Mill Street, Mira looked at Wren the way she had looked at them every morning for two years, past their shoulder, at the thing standing there in their coat. Then at Wren.

"Is it bad today?" she said.

Wren thought about it. It was an honest question, and they gave it an honest amount of thought.

"Yes," said Wren.

It was the first time they had ever said it to her. Not *just tired*. Not *I'm fine*. The word just came, plainly, in Wren's own voice, the way it would have come out of anyone.

Mira nodded. She didn't try to fix it. She'd stopped trying to fix it at the lamp-house.

"Do you want me to come up?" she said.

"Yes."

She came up. She walked them up Mill Street and across the square, where Oren was up his ladder at the lamp and the Hound was barking at the moon, and up Cobble Lane past Ada's dark window, and up the stairs. She sat on the edge of the bed in her cut-down coat while Wren lay facing the wall. She held Wren's good hand. She talked, a bit, about nothing: the works, her father, the cat that had had kittens in Wren's old boots, which had grown up and left and were now the problem of the whole street. The Mender sat on the floor by the bed with its empty hands in its lap. The guardian sat in the chair by the window, in Wren's coat, with Wren's face.

Wren fell asleep at about eleven. Mira stayed until nearly one, watching them breathe.

Then she bent down and kissed Wren's hair, and said, very low, "Cup'll be on the sill," and went home, because the works opened at six and her father was old now and somebody had to open it.

The guardian watched her go.

Then it got up out of the chair by the window, and came over, and sat down on the end of the bed.

In the dark, the clock ticked, as if it had been paid by the tick.

The room was cold.

Wren was in the bed, lying on their side, facing the wall.

### Chapter 30: After

> ◆ **GAME** · **EPILOGUE (playable, short).** Walking only, as Mira, across the rest of the winter and into spring. No combat, no HUD, no music. A sequence of short scenes, each ending in a cut to black. About fifteen minutes.

The Hound howled for three nights. Oren sat up with it on his back step, both nights after the first, with a blanket round the two of them. He didn't try to stop it. Nobody on the street complained.

---

They buried Wren on the hill above the church, in frozen ground, on a Thursday. The works closed for the day, for the first time in forty years. Callow stood at the graveside in his good coat, and wept, and didn't blame it on anything.

People came. More than Mira would have guessed. Mrs Penhallow, and her hen, which didn't count anything. The Abney boys, in collars. Bram Hollis, with his small grey terrier, who said Wren had always said hello. Oren, with the Hound pressed against his legs. Old men from the Crown and Anchor, and their herons. People Wren had made laugh for a sentence and then left before the second one, who had remembered the one sentence for years.

*Nobody will notice if you don't get up.* Wren had told her, at the lamp-house, that the voice said it every morning. Mira thought about that, standing on the hill. There were sixty people on it.

Ada came. Someone had brought her. She stood at the graveside in her coat, with her folded heron at her feet, and when the vicar had finished she said, "Oh, that's a shame," pleasantly, the way she'd say it about the weather.

But she didn't go when the others went.

She stood there in the snow after everyone had gone down the hill, and after the gravediggers had gone, with her hands in her coat pockets, looking at the ground. She stood there for an hour. Mira went back up for her in the end and took her arm, and Ada came, quite easily, and said, "Thank you, love."

She never said why she'd stayed. Mira didn't think she knew.

The heron didn't move. Mira watched it, all the way down the hill. It never did.

---

Nothing happened to the town.

Severine had been afraid, once, that something would. Nothing did. No grid came up out of the cobbles on Cobble Lane. The room didn't go to pencil. The Fade went on exactly as it had before Wren was born, a little thinner every year, the way the world gets old. The blue came out of the vat two shades paler and dried paler still. Wren had given four people their lives back and it had not made the slightest difference to the colour of anything.

Mira wrote to the Stillhouse. She kept it short. A fortnight later a reply came, in the small straight hand, and said that Mother Severine was very sorry, and would pray that Wren was at peace.

Mira burned it. She'd seen what peace looked like. It was forty glass bowls on a scrubbed floor.

---

Word came from Velle in January, in a three-week-old copy of the *Evening Courier* that someone had wrapped fish in.

*JUNIPER ASKE COLLAPSES ON STAGE*, it said. In the middle of the hundred and ninety-ninth performance of *The Queen of Ashes*, in the second act, she had sat down on the floor of the stage and not got up. She had not said her line. She had put her hands flat against her stomach and bent over and, the paper said, made a sound. The audience had made a sound back. The season was cancelled.

The date was the evening of the day Wren died.

Mira read it twice before she understood. When a host died, the gods they carried went home. Juniper had said no. She had said *please*. Wren had closed a door on its fingers to keep it from her. And it had gone home anyway, on the first morning there was no one left to hold it.

Mira put the paper in the stove.

---

A letter came for Wren in February, care of Callow's Dye Works, Lowmarrow, in a quick, slanting hand that had been written on a knee.

> *Wren —*
>
> *Found her. Coast, like I thought. She's alive. She's not well. Not well at all, if I'm honest, and her mum says she's been worse. But she's alive and she opened the door to me, which she didn't have to.*
>
> *I'm staying. Not one night. I've unpacked the bag. All three of them.*
>
> *I wanted you to know you did that. You'll say you didn't. You did.*
>
> *— T.*

Mira read it on the step of the works, in the cold, with the Mender beside her. Then she sat with it for a long time.

She thought about writing back. She thought about how to put it. Every way she could think of had Tess standing on a doorstep on the coast reading it, with her friend inside, not well. In the end she didn't write. She thought that one day she would. Not yet.

---

In March there was a poster on the gable end of the house opposite Wren's.

Good white paper. The only white thing in the street.

**THE ORDER OF THE STILL HAND**
**RECKONING: TODAY, TOWN HALL**
**ALL WELCOME · NO CHARGE · NO SHAME**

Someone had drawn a small smiling sun in the corner.

Mira passed it on the way to the works. She passed the town hall too. The line went out of the door and down the steps, longer than last time, and the two Stillers in white stood at the door with their hands folded. The assessor at the desk inside was the same young man as before. His hand was quite all right now. Mira could see him through the door, smiling, reaching two fingers out to the god of whoever was at the front.

She didn't stop. She didn't look at the faces in the line. She went to the works and opened it, because it was six o'clock.

---

She stopped putting the cup out in April.

She didn't decide to. One morning she forgot, and when she noticed, halfway through the morning, she didn't go and do it. The next morning she didn't either. That was all.

The Mender sat at the long table in the vat room, these days, with its hands in its lap. It had always been fixing something. There didn't seem to be anything it knew how to fix.

---

She went up to Wren's room once more, at the end of April, to clear it, because Ada had been asked about it and had said she didn't mind either way.

The copybook was in the coat on the chair, where Wren had put it. She'd known it would be. She took it out and opened it, and went through the birds, all of them, and came to the kitchen, and the woman at the sink, and the child on the stairs. *Mam is sad. Tomorrow I will tell the town hall.* And then the blank pages after.

She sat on the edge of the bed for a long time.

Then she looked up at the wall.

She'd never known what they were. Wren had said once it was a ledger, and laughed, the way Wren laughed at things so that you wouldn't ask the second question. She put her hand flat against them. It was only a wall, and only cold. She thought about counting them, and didn't. She didn't want to know the number. She thought that whatever it was, it would be too small.

She took the copybook and the white sash. She left the wall. She went down the stairs and past Ada on the step and down the lane.

---

In the evening Oren came round the lamps.

He did it every evening now, with his long pole on his shoulder, the way he'd done it for thirty years before. The Hound went ahead of him down every street, barking at everything. It barked less than it used to. Oren was tired. He was always tired now. The Hound still woke him four times a night, and some nights he still went to the end of his yard and looked up Cobble Lane at a window with no light in it before he remembered.

Mira was on the step of the works when he came by. He stopped. The Hound sat down at her feet without being asked and put its head on her knee.

Oren looked at her for a moment with his big worried face. Then he asked her the question she had asked Wren every morning for two years at the gate, and once, at the end, been answered.

"Is it bad today?"

Mira thought about it. It was an honest question, and she gave it an honest amount of thought.

"Yes," she said.

Oren nodded.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't try to fix it. He sat down on the step beside her, with the Hound between them, and they sat there together while the light went, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

When it was properly dark, Oren got up, and picked up his pole, and went on round the lamps.
