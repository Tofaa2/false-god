## ACT THREE: THE UNFINISHED

### Chapter 18: Severine's Study

Severine already knew. Wren saw it on her face in the courtyard. For the first time since the white road, she wasn't smiling.

"The watchers on the south road sent word," she said. "Two days ago. A waystation. And a sheep farm at Crane Moss. And a mile of the Corran road." She looked at Wren for a long moment. "Come up."

> ◆ **GAME** · Severine's study unlocks. It is the **only room in the Stillhouse with any clutter**: books stacked on the floor, a cold cup on the windowsill, a moth-eaten armchair, a small coal fire. Wren and the player see it for the first time with the guardian silent and the pencil-scratch audio ever-present.

It was a small room at the top of the west tower, and it was the only room in the Stillhouse that looked lived in. There were books on the floor in stacks. There was a moth-eaten armchair with a blanket over the back, and a little coal fire, and a kettle on a hob. There was a teacup on the windowsill with a skin on it, forgotten. Wren looked at the teacup and something in their chest turned over, and they didn't know why.

Severine made tea, properly, the way the Order didn't: strong, with milk, in two mismatched cups. She set one in front of Wren, then sat in the armchair and looked at them over hers.

"Roll up your sleeve, please," she said. "The left."

Wren did.

The gap ran the whole length of the arm now. From the shoulder down to the wrist, the outline of Wren simply wasn't there, and where Wren should have been, there was paper. Clean, white, blank paper, with nothing on it at all. And at the edges of the paper, where Wren's own line stopped, there were colours. Faint. Bleeding in at the edges, like dye at the edge of a wet cloth. Warm orange. Glass blue. A deep, sodden green. Colours that weren't Wren's.

Severine looked at it for a long time. She didn't touch it.

"You've been carrying five other people," she said. "Five whole gods. All their weight. No one is drawn to hold that much, Wren. No one ever was. It's in the grain of you now." She set down her cup. "It won't come out."

"What does that mean?"

Severine was quiet.

"How long," said Wren.

"Not long." Severine met their eyes, and didn't look away, and Wren would always be grateful for that, afterwards. "A season. Perhaps less. Less if you keep fighting. Every time you call on them, you'll open a little further."

Wren sat with the cup in their hands. The tea was very good. It was the first thing Wren had tasted properly, really tasted, since the Quiet Room. The scratching went on in the walls, and in the floor, and in the sky outside the window, steady and soft.

"There's one way," Severine said gently.

Wren looked up.

"Let me finish what we started." She leaned forward in her armchair. "Not only the god. That's past doing; it's in the paper now, and I don't know how to reach it there. But *you*. Let me Still the rest of you, Wren. Everything. All of it. If there's nothing left in you that struggles, the gods you're carrying will settle. They'll go quiet. They'll stop pulling you apart. And the underdrawing —" she glanced at the window, at the faint grid drawing itself across the white sky, "— the underdrawing comes out of *you*. It's your struggle, spreading. Still you, and it stops. Everything stops." She paused. "You'd live. A long time, I think."

"As what?"

"As Oren is. As your mother is." Severine said it simply. "As I am."

Wren looked at her.

"I was twelve," Severine said. "When I did it. My god used to tell me I'd be better off not here, and I was twelve, and I believed it. I believed it with my whole heart. And I went to an old Stiller in the town where I grew up, and I asked her to make it stop, and she did." She looked into her tea. "I haven't been frightened since. Not once. Fifty years. I've had a good, useful life, and I've helped thousands of people, and I have never once lain awake. It isn't nothing, Wren. I know you think it's nothing. It isn't. I promise you it isn't."

Wren thought about a doorstep at the top of Cobble Lane. A bowl of peas. *That'll be nice.* And under that, newer, a kitchen with the tap running, and an old woman in white leaning over a desk to see a child.

"Then who'd be living it?" Wren said.

Severine didn't answer that for a while.

"No one would be suffering it," she said at last. "I've always thought that was the more important thing."

She stood, and went to the window, and stood with her back to Wren, looking south over the white road, at the grid creeping up the sky.

"I won't force it," she said. "Not today. I've never Stilled anyone who didn't ask, and I'm too old to start." She was quiet. "But that —" she nodded at the sky, "— reached a waystation this week. Next month it'll reach a village. And I have watched what happens to people inside it, Wren. I've had the reports. They don't suffer. They don't feel anything. They just get *fainter*. And then they're not there." She turned round. "If it reaches a town, I'll come. Wherever you are. With the whole Order, if I have to. And I'll do it whether you ask me or not. Because I'd rather you hate me than let a town be rubbed out."

She meant it. Wren could see that she meant every word, and that it cost her, and that she would do it anyway.

"Thank you," said Wren. "For telling me."

"Where will you go?"

Wren didn't know. Wren stood up and rolled down their sleeve over the white paper of their arm.

"Home, I suppose," said Wren. "Everyone does."

Severine smiled at that, a little, sadly.

"Yes," she said. "Everyone does, at the end."

### Chapter 19: The Gate, Again

It was snowing at the gate. The first snow.

Wren came down the long white steps of the Stillhouse with Oren behind them and the scratching all around them in the snow. Wren was trying to remember the way south. Trying to remember anything. And there was someone standing at the end of the white road in the snow, small and dark against all that white, and for a moment Wren thought it was a Stiller, and then that it was a post, and then they saw the red.

The thread. It came in across the white flagstones from the south, as it always had, Wren's own thread, unwinding behind Wren's own feet. But it didn't stop at Wren. It went on past them, down the steps, through the gate, and away down the white road. And there, at the gate, someone was holding it.

Not holding. *Winding* it. There was a figure beside her made of darning wool, with the thread running into its hands and the spool of its own chest growing as it wound, slowly, steadily, hand over hand, as she walked.

She'd followed it. All the way. Three hundred miles of thread, backwards, from a gatepost at the bottom of Lowmarrow to here. She had written that she would come if she didn't hear, and Wren had read the letter out loud in a pleasant voice and put it in a drawer.

Mira's coat was torn at the hem. Her boots were wrecked; one of them was tied together with thread. Her hair had come loose from its bit of string and was white with snow. She saw Wren, and stopped.

She didn't run. Wren would remember that. She walked the last part steadily, carefully, the way you walk toward someone in the road who might bolt.

> ◆ **GAME** · Cutscene. **Mira joins the party** for the rest of the game as a second companion alongside Oren. She is not a combatant, but the Mender acts in the world (see Chapter 21). Wren's companion banter in Act 3 changes completely: Mira talks, Oren listens, and Wren's dialogue wheel begins, slowly, to offer options in Wren's own ink.

"You stopped answering," said Mira.

"I know."

"Fourteen," she said. "I counted."

"I read them."

She was quiet. "When?"

Wren couldn't find the words. Wren tried twice and they didn't come.

"After," Wren said. "When I couldn't — Mira, I read them when it didn't matter. When I couldn't feel them. I read every one of them out loud and I didn't feel anything at all. I read the last one and I said *that's nice*." Wren's voice went. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

She looked at Wren. She looked properly, for a long time, in the snow: at the white sash with its silver edge, at the grid lines faint in the snow around Wren's boots, at the stillness in Wren's face that hadn't quite gone. And then her eyes went to Wren's left side, where the coat hung wrong. As though there were less of Wren inside it than there should be.

"What's that?" she said.

Wren told her.

> ◆ **GAME** · The conversation plays in **a single held wide shot**: the two of them small against the white wall of the Stillhouse in falling snow, no music, dialogue mixed so quiet the player has to lean in. Oren stands a little way off, catching snowflakes on his sleeve.

Wren told her all of it. Severine's study. The paper in the arm. *A season. Perhaps less.* Wren told it plainly, because there didn't seem to be any other way, and Mira listened, and didn't interrupt, and didn't look away once.

When Wren had finished, Mira didn't say anything. She stood very still in the snow.

The Mender moved first.

It stepped forward past Mira's shoulder, and reached out its long woollen hand, needle-finger raised, toward Wren's left side. And before Wren could step back, it had taken hold of the edge of the gap, where Wren's line stopped and the paper began. And it began to sew.

"Mira —" said Wren.

"Let it," said Mira.

It sewed very fast. Red thread. Small, neat, tight stitches, one after another, down from the shoulder, drawing the two edges of Wren's line back together like the edges of a torn sleeve. Wren felt every one of them. They didn't hurt. They felt like being held too tightly by someone who is frightened.

Where the stitches closed, colour came back.

Wren looked down and watched it happen. Wren's own colour, the faded brown and grey of their coat and the tired pink of their hand, coming back into the arm under the red stitches like warmth coming back into cold fingers. The Mender sewed all the way to the wrist, and tied off, and bit the thread.

For the first time in Wren's life, their outline was whole. Closed. All the way round.

In the walls of the Stillhouse, and in the snow, the scratching stopped.

It stopped for perhaps three seconds. Then it went on, a little slower than before, the way a person writes when they've thought of something.

"There," said Mira. Her voice cracked in the middle of it and she kept going anyway. "There. You see? *There.*"

### Chapter 20: Corran, Again

> ◆ **GAME** · Wren's **kit at the start of Act 3**: all five abilities, plus **Stitches**, a temporary bonus to maximum health from the Mender's sewing, renewed at each lamp and eroded by every fight. **The underdrawing spreads from Wren in a large radius** as they travel. NPCs within it go to pencil. **Stiller patrols** now roam the roads. The guardian is silent; the pencil-scratch audio is constant.

They went south, three of them now, with the Mender paying the thread out behind them as they walked. Oren lit the lamps. Mira sewed. Wren walked in the middle and tried not to look behind them, where the world went to grid.

Corran was worse than it had been on the way north to the Stillhouse. The furnaces were still going in their long brick sheds, but the glass they lit was grey, and the great green spire was a pale ghost of itself, and the people in the streets were faint, faint pencil, barely there at all. Wren walked down the high street with the underdrawing spreading out around them, and watched a woman sweeping her step go from ink to pencil to almost nothing as Wren passed, and keep sweeping, and smile.

Ambrose Pell's workshop door was open. The furnace was going.

The floor was clean.

All eleven years of it had been swept away. The broken glass was gone. The floor was bare scrubbed boards, and on it, in rows, all down the length of the workshop, on every bench and every shelf and every inch of floor, were finished pieces. Bowls. Vases. Goblets. A great dish. A tall slender bottle like a heron. A lamp. Dozens of them. Forty, perhaps more. Every one of them perfect. Every one of them flawless, thin as a soap bubble, without a seed or a streak or a ripple anywhere. Every one of them as dead as a photograph.

Ambrose was at the bench, finishing another. He saw Wren in the doorway, and his face lit with polite, pleasant recognition.

"Ah," he said. "It's you. Come in. Look." He swept an arm out over the rows and rows of glass. "Look how many."

Wren looked. Mira, behind them in the doorway, put her hand over her mouth.

"They're lovely," said Wren.

"Are they?" Ambrose looked at them. He picked up the nearest, a goblet, the colour of weak tea, and turned it in the furnace-light. It was perfect. He looked at it the way you'd look at a word you've repeated so many times it's stopped meaning anything. "Do you think they're any good?" he asked. And then, apologetically: "I can't tell, you see. I used to be able to tell. I'd look at a thing and I'd *know*. Now I just —" He set the goblet down. "Well. They're finished. That's what I wanted. I wanted to finish something."

He came over and held out his hand to Wren, kindly, the way a host does to a guest.

"I'm glad you came back," said Ambrose. "I never got to thank you."

Wren took his hand.

> ◆ **GAME** · **THE FIRST RETURN (unprompted).** Cutscene. It just happens. **TRUE EDGE is lost from the kit immediately** (UI: the ability icon cracks and goes grey).

It went before Wren knew it was going.

It was the stitches. Or it was the gap under them. Wren never knew which. They only felt it: something cold and clear and sharp, deep in their chest, lifting its head. Turning, like a compass needle, toward the hand Wren was holding. And then going, quick and bright, the way a fish goes out of your hands back into a river. Down Wren's arm. Out through the gap in the line, under the red stitches, which parted for it like a curtain. Into Ambrose.

Ambrose staggered.

He let go of Wren's hand and caught at the bench. His eyes went wide. The glass god came up behind him in a rush of light, all its plates at once, amber and green and that clear blue you could see the fire through, armoured and enormous and cracked down one shoulder. It stood behind him the way it always had. It looked down at him. And then it turned its glass face and looked at the workshop. At the rows and rows of perfect, finished glass.

Ambrose looked too.

He looked for a long time. And his face changed, in the way Oren's had changed in the square, only backwards: a hand closing into a fist. The lines came back into it one by one. The worry. The grief. The eleven years.

"Oh, *God,*" said Ambrose Pell. "Oh, God, they're *awful.*"

He picked up the goblet the colour of tea, and looked at it, and threw it at the wall. It burst into a thousand pieces. He picked up the dish. The heron-bottle. The lamp. One after another, he went down the rows and broke them all, every one, not angrily, not wildly, just steadily, the way you'd weed a bed. Glass on the floor again. Glass everywhere. Forty pieces, perfect and dead, shattered on the scrubbed boards.

And then, at the very end of the last row, he stopped.

It was the green bowl. The first one, the one he'd made the night after Wren took his god. Thin as a bubble, the colour of green water. He picked it up, and turned it, and Wren saw his face: and it wasn't grief, and it wasn't anger. It was attention. The furious, total attention of a man looking at his own work.

"There," Ambrose said, very quietly. "*There*. That's wrong. Look at the lip. You see the lip? It's dead. It's dead because I didn't care." He was crying, and didn't seem to notice. "But there, in the foot. In the foot. That's — that's nearly something. I can see where it's nearly something."

Behind him, the Ninth Draft had started, very slowly, to polish the crack on its shoulder.

"It's not right," said Ambrose, to the bowl, to the god, to Wren. "It's not right at all. I'll make the next one." He set the bowl down on the bench, gently, the only piece left in the room. "I'll make the next one, and it'll be wrong too." He laughed, wet and astonished. "God help me. I'd forgotten how much I hate this."

---

They stood outside in the street. Wren didn't remember coming out. Mira had a hand on their arm. Oren was beside them, looking with mild interest at the glass dust on his boots.

Wren looked down at the street.

The cobbles under Wren's feet were ink.

Not grid. Not pencil. Ink. Thin, tired, faded Corran ink, but ink, real, the lines drawn in, the stones with their shading. A yard of it around Wren's boots. And beyond that yard, the grid went on, faint blue squares out across the street, and the sweeping woman on her step was still pencil. But around Wren, the underdrawing had pulled *in*. Like a tide going out. A yard. A yard and a half.

Mira saw it too. Her hand tightened on Wren's arm.

"It's going back," she said. "Wren. It's going *back*. If you give them all back —" She stopped herself, the way people stop themselves on the edge of hoping out loud. Then she said it anyway. "If you give them all back, maybe it closes. Maybe it was only ever the weight."

Wren looked back over their shoulder.

The guardian was standing in the middle of the street.

It hadn't been there a moment before. It had come up out of the paper, the way it had come up out of the floor of the Quiet Room, only it wasn't enormous now. It wasn't wearing anything. No lantern, no glass, no letters, no weed, no veil. A thin, tall, faceless thing in the middle of a grey street, with a crosshair where its face should be.

It was exactly Wren's height.

And its lines were darker than Wren remembered. Not the faint grey under-marks of the town hall. Something heavier. Something nearer to ink.

It looked at Wren. Wren looked at it. And for the first time since a white room at the bottom of a white stair, it spoke.

*You gave one back,* it said.

It wasn't angry. It sounded bewildered. It sounded like someone who has woken up in a strange room.

*That'll make you weaker.*

"Yes," said Wren.

*They'll catch you. The Order. You heard her. You're going to be weaker, and they're going to come, and you won't be able to —*

"Probably."

*Then why would you —*

"Because I took them," said Wren.

The guardian didn't say anything to that.

It stood in the street with its crosshair face turned toward Wren, and the grey grid around it, and it said nothing at all. It had had something to say about everything for fifteen years. It had never once had anything to say to that.

Mira looked from Wren to the thing in the street. She couldn't hear it. She could see it, as anyone could see a god: something tall and grey standing in the road, and Wren talking to it.

"What's it saying?" she asked.

"Nothing," said Wren. "It's not saying anything."

### Chapter 21: Stitches

Every night, at the lamps, the Mender sewed Wren closed.

Oren would find a bracket and light the lamp, and sit down under it, and fall asleep. And Mira would sit beside Wren and say, "Sleeve," and Wren would roll it up. And the Mender would take out its needle-finger and begin. The stitches from the night before would be gone by then, or nearly: torn, pulled loose, unravelled, the red thread trailing off Wren's arm in little frayed ends. The gap would be open again underneath, white and clean. The Mender would pick out the old thread, patiently, and start again from the shoulder.

> ◆ **GAME** · **STITCHES (mechanic).** Resting at a lamp has the Mender sew Wren's gap: a temporary **bonus to maximum health**. Stitches **erode with every hit taken and every ability used**; the bonus visibly frays off the health bar. Each lamp sews fewer and fewer of them. **First region of Act 3:** stitches last a full in-game day. **By the third:** they tear by midday. **By the last:** they barely survive a single fight. Players will come to rely on them, just as Mira does.

The first week, the stitches held all day.

The second week, they tore by noon. Wren felt it happen, out on the road, a soft unpicking down the left side. Like a seam giving way. Mira felt it too. She'd see Wren's face, and her own face would go very still, and she wouldn't say anything until they reached the lamp that night. And then, "Sleeve," and the Mender would start again. Double stitches, this time. Two rows.

The third week, they tore by the middle of the morning.

Mira started sewing at midday as well. They'd stop on the road, and she'd make Wren sit on a wall, and the Mender would sew. Three rows, now. Four. The stitches got smaller. Then they got bigger, and faster, and messier, crooked, the thread knotting. The Mender's long woollen fingers began to shake. And the thread was thinner. Wren noticed that before Mira did. It wasn't the deep wet red it had been at the gate. It was the red of the baker's awning now, the scar-pink. The Mender was running low on itself.

So was Mira. She was sleeping two hours a night. She was eating whatever Oren put in her hands and not noticing what it was.

"Mira," said Wren, on a wall outside Dunmere, with the fourth row going in. "Mira, you're —"

"I'm nearly done."

"You're unpicking your own coat."

She was. The thread had run out. And the Mender had begun, without stopping, without being asked, to unpick the hem of Mira's coat and use that instead. Brown wool. Grey. The lining. Whatever came.

"It doesn't matter," said Mira. "I've got another coat at home."

They went on.

> ◆ **GAME** · **The guardian's interference turns outward during Act 3.** Without explanation, it begins to **protect** Wren: it rubs out a bridge just ahead of a Stiller patrol, draws a doorway into a wall at the end of a dead-end alley, tilts a roof so a Warden's charge slides off. Players who've spent two acts hating its edits will read this as the guardian softening. **It isn't.** It is keeping anyone else from taking what it means to finish itself. **Watchlight** still shows its edits glowing, and they now glow in a dark, steady line, closer to ink than pencil.

There were Stillers on the roads now. Severine had kept her word, as far as Wren could tell; nobody had come for Wren yet. But they were out. Pairs of them, in white, on the crossroads and at the bridges, watching the grid spread across the sky to the south. Twice Wren saw them in the distance and went round. Once they couldn't go round.

It was on the Velle road, in a cutting between two hills. Two Stillers in white, coming the other way, a hundred yards off. They saw Wren. They saw the grid on the road around Wren, and the sash, and they stopped. One of them raised a hand.

And the bridge between them, an old stone bridge over a stream at the bottom of the cutting, rubbed itself out.

Wren watched it go. One stroke at a time, from the Stillers' side. Parapet, arch, keystone, gone. A gap of empty paper over a stream, and the Stillers on the far side of it, looking at the gap, looking at Wren.

Wren looked round.

The guardian was standing on the bank behind them. It had come up out of the paper again. Its long hand was raised toward the bridge. It lowered it.

*They'd have taken you,* it said. It sounded almost defensive. *Before you'd finished.*

Wren looked at it for a long time.

"Thank you," said Wren.

The guardian turned its crosshair face away. It didn't answer.

That night, at a lamp on a fencepost on the high moor, the Mender sewed for two hours.

Wren lay on their back on the heather with their arm out on Mira's knee, and the Mender sewed. The stitches went in and tore out. Went in and tore out. Wren could feel each one go: a pull, and a give, and nothing. It wasn't holding. It wasn't holding at all now. And the Mender, with Mira's coat-hem unravelling in its fingers, wouldn't stop.

It began to sew other things.

It sewed Wren's sleeve to the heather. It sewed the cuff of Wren's coat to the hem of Mira's skirt. It reached out, past Wren, past the lamp, and sewed Oren's bootlace to the fencepost while he slept. It sewed the grass. It began, in a little frantic flurry of crooked brown and grey, to sew the dark around the lamp, small knots and loops of wool hanging in the empty night as though there were something there to hold together.

Its long woollen fingers were shaking so much the needle kept missing.

"It's all right," said Mira. She was sewing too, now, with her own hands, with a needle from her bag. Her face was white. "It's all right, it's nearly — it's *nearly* —"

"Mira."

"It's *nearly* holding."

Wren looked up past her, at the dark.

The guardian was sitting on the other side of the lamp. Just at the edge of the light. It was sitting cross-legged in the heather, the way Wren sat, and it had Wren's coat on now. Not traced, the way it had traced the Hound's lantern and the Draft's glass in the training yard. Drawn. The same coat, with the same split in the cuff that the Mender had darned at the gate the night they left. It was watching the Mender. It watched it sew the grass, and sew the air, and miss with the needle, and sew again.

*She's going to run out,* it said, very quietly. Not cruelly. The way you'd point out that it was getting dark.

It watched the Mender for a long time.

*And then what will she have left?*

### Chapter 22: Velle, Again

Velle had gone quiet.

Not entirely. It could never go entirely quiet. But they came into it at night, as everyone did, and it wasn't the roar it had been. The lanterns were lit, the red and gold and violet, but there were fewer of them. The playbills on the walls had faded. The barkers were outside the theatres, and their voices didn't carry. And there was a faint blue grid laid over the whole lower town, like frost, where the white road had come down off the hills.

Wren had been to three of the theatres on the hill by midnight, and they were all closed for the season. *Patrons unwell,* said one sign. *The company regrets.*

The Lyceum was open.

> ◆ **GAME** · **Velle, revisited.** The city is visibly paler, and the underdrawing radius around Wren swallows whole streets. Fewer NPCs, and quieter. Any player who returned to Velle in Act 2's optional content will notice what has gone. The Lyceum is open; the guardian is silent here.

*The Queen of Ashes* was still running. Its hundred and ninetieth performance, said the board outside. The house was nearly full. Wren and Mira bought tickets in the gods, the cheap seats at the very top, and climbed up through the dark, and sat, and watched it.

It was perfect.

It was the same play. Every line was exactly where it had been. Every gesture was placed. Juniper was the Queen, and she was magnificent, in exactly the way a perfect statue of a woman is magnificent. She was furious and tender and terrible, all on cue. And in the second act, when her son betrayed her, she sat down on the floor of the stage and said, *"Oh,"* very precisely.

Nobody in the audience made a sound.

Between the scenes, Wren watched the wings. Juniper came off and stood in the shadow of the flats, in the same place. She didn't turn her back. She didn't press her hands to her stomach. She stood, and waited for her cue, and went on.

They applauded at the end. They applauded politely, steadily, for exactly as long as was right, and then they got up and went home.

---

She was in her dressing room. She remembered Wren this time, or seemed to. She smiled at Wren in the mirror, a pleasant, unremarkable smile. She was taking off her face with cold cream.

"The Warden," she said. "How nice. You've lost your sash."

Wren had. They'd taken it off on the road outside Corran and left it on a wall.

"I've come to give you something back," said Wren.

Juniper looked at Wren in the mirror. She went on taking off her face, slowly, with a cloth.

"I know," she said.

Wren hadn't expected that.

"I've heard," she said. "It's Velle, darling. Everyone hears everything. The young person from the Order who's going round the country undoing things. The glassblower in Corran. It was in the *Evening Courier*. There was a drawing." She put down the cloth. "I wondered if you'd come here."

"Do you want her?" said Wren. "The Veiled?"

Juniper turned round on her stool.

She didn't answer straight away. She looked at Wren, and then past Wren, at Mira in the doorway, and then she looked at Wren's left side. It was hanging oddly under the coat, where the stitches were torn again.
