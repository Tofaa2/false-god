### Chapter 25: Hollin's Lamp

The last lamp before Lowmarrow was an old lamp-house on a hill above the river road. It was a little round stone tower where, a hundred years ago, a keeper had lived to tend the beacon that guided the carts down off the moor. The roof was half gone. The beacon was a rusted cage. From the doorway you could see down into the valley, and the town at the bottom of it, grey on grey, the Midwinter Lamp a black pin in the middle of the square, and a few windows lit.

Two days' walk. One, if Wren could still walk properly, which they couldn't.

Oren lit the beacon. He went up the inside stair with his pole, and fiddled in the rusted cage, and found something that would burn, and lit it, and came down. Then he lay down on his back by the hearth with his hands folded on his chest and went to sleep.

Mira sat by Wren in the doorway. The Mender sat behind her, with the hem of her coat in its fingers, and it was already unpicking.

Wren put a hand over the Mender's.

Its long woollen fingers stopped. The needle stopped. The Mender looked at Wren, with no face to look with.

"Stop," said Wren. "Please."

"It's nearly holding," said Mira.

"It's not. It held for an hour today." Wren said it gently. "An hour, Mira. Less. It went at the stile." Wren looked at her. "You can't sew me shut."

Mira's face did something terrible. It crumpled, and she fought it, and it crumpled anyway, and she was furious and crying both at once, the way she'd been at the gate of the Stillhouse but worse.

"Then what am I supposed to *do?*" she said. "I walked three hundred miles, Wren. I followed a *thread*. I'm not — you don't get to just — I'm supposed to *do* something."

"I know."

"So what do I *do?*"

"I don't know." Wren looked down at the valley, at the town, at the dark pin of the Lamp. "Can I tell you what it says?"

Mira went very still.

"You've seen it," Wren said. "Your whole life. Behind me. Everyone in Lowmarrow's seen it, looking over my shoulder, the way people do. But you can't hear it. Nobody can hear it. You never once asked what it says." Wren took a breath. "I've never told anyone. Not in fifteen years. Not my mother. Not the Order. I'd like to tell you."

Mira sat down. Properly, on the stone step, beside Wren, facing them.

"All right," she said.

"It says nobody will notice if I don't get up," Wren said.

It came out strange. Wren had heard it so many times, in that other voice, older and flatter, from just behind the shoulder. Wren had never heard it in their own voice before, out loud, in the cold air. It sounded smaller.

Mira didn't answer straight away. When she did, she said it quite steadily.

"I was going to come and knock," she said. "That first morning. I had my coat on." She looked at Wren. "Every morning, Wren. I had my coat on every single morning for two years. I used to stand at the gate with my coat on and give you five more minutes. And then five more. And you always came. You always came round the corner of Mill Street just before I went to get you."

Wren hadn't known that.

"It says you only ask because you're kind," Wren said. "Whether it's bad. It says you'd ask the goat."

"I do ask the goat," said Mira. "I don't ask the goat how it takes its tea."

Wren laughed. It hurt. It surprised them both.

"It said you'd cut the thread," said Wren. "Inside a month. It said everyone does."

Mira held up her left hand.

The end of the red thread was tied round her wrist. Not the end the Mender had been winding. The other end. The first end, from the gatepost. She must have untied it from the gate before she set out, and tied it on herself. It had been there the whole way, under her sleeve, three hundred miles.

Wren looked at it for a long time.

"It said letters are easy to be kind in," said Wren. "It said wait until you have to look at me."

"I'm looking at you," said Mira.

She was. She hadn't looked away once.

"It says I hurt everyone who stands near me," said Wren. "It said I couldn't even get out of a line without hurting someone. It said you'd got enough to carry without me."

"Probably true," said Mira, "the first two." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "And the third one isn't up to you."

"It says you'll leave," said Wren. "When it gets bad. When you see."

Mira didn't say anything to that one for a while. She looked down at the valley, and the town, and back at Wren. At the paper side of Wren, the coat hanging off it. At the frayed red stitches.

"Is this not bad?" she said, very quietly.

Wren had nothing to say to that. Neither did the voice.

Wren went on. It took a long time. There were more of them than Wren had thought: fifteen years of them, stacked up, a whole ledger. *You're very funny with strangers. You know why? Because they leave.* "I'm not a stranger." *He follows you because he has nowhere else to be.* "He follows you because his hands know where the lamps go, and you're one of them. Ask him." *You'll make her watch.* "I'd rather watch than not." They went on, and Mira answered every one. Some of them she couldn't answer, and said so, and that turned out to be an answer as well.

At the edge of the firelight, in the corner of the lamp-house, the guardian sat listening.

It had come up out of the floor while Wren was talking. Wren had seen it come, out of the corner of an eye, and hadn't stopped. It was small now, the size of a child of ten or eleven, sitting with its pencil knees drawn up and its arms round them, in the shadow by the wall. And it was listening. It was listening to its own words, fifteen years of them, said out loud in Wren's voice to someone else. And it was listening to someone answer them.

With every one, it got a little smaller.

When Wren finally stopped, because there were no more, or none that mattered, the guardian was the size of a child of seven. It sat in the corner and didn't move. Its crosshair face was turned toward Mira.

*They sound different,* it said, very quietly. *Out loud.*

"Yes," said Wren. "They do."

---

Mira took the Mender's needle-hand in hers.

The Mender let her. It had stopped shaking. Together, slowly, she and her god began to unpick the last row of stitches down Wren's arm. One at a time. The thread came out of the edges of Wren's line, and the gap opened under it, paper-white, edged with faint other people's colours, orange and the dim held-breath grey of the Veiled. Mira looked at it the whole time. She didn't look away.

When the last stitch was out, she wound the thread round her fingers and put it in her pocket.

"All right," she said.

"All right?"

"All right." She looked at the gap. "Then I'll just be here."

"That's not much," said Wren.

"It's what I've got."

She kissed Wren.

It was brief, and clumsy, and her nose was cold, and Wren's mouth missed slightly at first. It was exactly the way it is between two people who've waited two years, standing in a works yard every morning with a cup between them, both certain the other one would never.

In the corner, the guardian turned round, quite politely, and sat facing the wall.

---

They slept side by side on the floor of the lamp-house, under the beacon, in their coats. Wren didn't sleep well. Neither did Mira. Wren could hear her not sleeping. They lay there in the dark, both awake, listening to Oren breathe and the beacon tick and the wind on the moor. They both stayed.

### Chapter 26: Lowmarrow

They came down into Lowmarrow on Midwinter Eve, in the late afternoon, with the snow coming.

The town was deep in the Fade now. Wren saw that as soon as they came off the moor road onto the bridge. It had been thin when they left; now it was barely there. The cobbles were a few faint lines. The shopfronts had lost their lettering. The houses were pale outlines with a door and two windows, like the houses on the white road. The baker's awning wasn't any colour at all. And the people in the streets were faint, and slow, and pleasant, and the gods walking beside them were small and grey.

But there were lanterns. Someone had strung lanterns across the square, the way they used to at Midwinter, and they weren't lit yet, and they had no colour. But they were there.

The knot was still on the dye works gatepost. Neat and tight, the way the Mender had tied it. The thread was gone from it; Mira had it on her wrist. But the knot was there. Wren put a hand on it as they went past.

Mira's father came out of the counting-house as they came through the yard. He was an old man now. He hadn't been, eight months ago. He looked at Mira, at her torn coat and her boots tied together with thread, and he looked at Wren, and he didn't say anything about either of them. He held his daughter for a long time in the snow.

"The blue took," he said, into her hair. "Tuesday. It took, Mira. It held."

---

Wren's mother was on her doorstep at the top of Cobble Lane, shelling nothing. There were no peas at Midwinter. She sat on the step with an empty bowl in her lap, and her folded grey god beside her, and watched the snow.

"Hello, love," said Ada Tolley. "You look tired."

"I am tired."

"It's a tired sort of face," she agreed pleasantly. Then she looked at Wren more carefully, with the faint interest that was the most she had. "You're thinner, love. On the one side."

"Yes."

"Mm." She looked at the snow. "I've missed you on Sundays."

She said it the way she'd say it looked like rain. But Wren sat down on the step beside her anyway, in the snow, with their paper side against the doorframe. Mira waited at the bottom of the lane.

"Mam," said Wren. "When I was born. What was it like? My god?"

Ada Tolley thought about it. It was a long way back. The Stilled could remember, Wren knew; it was only that remembering didn't seem to matter to them.

"Oh, it was a little bird," she said. "A little brown bird. Round, like. A scribble. With its tail stuck up." She smiled, pleasantly, at the snow. "Your father wanted to call you after his mother. But I looked at you in the cot with that little bird on the pillow, singing away, and I said, no. That's a wren, that is."

She shelled a pea that wasn't there.

"So we called you Wren," she said.

Wren sat on the step with her for a long time, in the snow. Then they leaned over, and kissed her cheek, and got up.

"That's nice," said Ada Tolley.

---

The room hadn't changed. Somebody, Mira probably, had been in and dusted. The clock had stopped. The box was still under the bed.

And the wall was still covered in marks.

Wren stood in the doorway and looked at it. Fifteen years of them. Thousands. Tens of thousands of faint pencil strokes in their little fenced groups of five, from the skirting board up as high as a person could reach lying down, and under the bed, and along the wall behind the wardrobe. Wren had never counted them. Wren had never read them. It was a ledger, and you didn't read the ledger. You let it pile up.

There was a new one. Near the bottom. Fainter than the others.

It had been drawn the morning Wren left. The morning of the Reckoning. Wren could even tell which one it was: the last in its row. Upright, careful. Not crossed through.

Wren looked at it for a long time.

Then they turned and went out, and went down to the square.

### Chapter 27: The Stillness

They came in from the north road, out of the snow, in white. Thirty of them. Forty. Severine walked at the front.

The people of Lowmarrow stood aside to let them through, pleasantly, and the Order came across the square and stopped in a long white line in front of the Lamp, and Severine came on alone. She stopped a few yards from Wren. There was snow on her shawl. She looked very tired.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"It hasn't reached a town," said Wren. "You said if it reached a town. Look." Wren gestured at the square: at the faint grid round their own boots, only a yard across now, and the ordinary pale cobbles beyond. "It's going. I've given them back. Four of them. It's going *in*."

"Yes," said Severine. "I know. I had reports from Corran. And Saltby. It's been extraordinary." She meant it. "But you're carrying one still. And it's tearing you. I can see it from here. And when you go, Wren —" She stopped. "When a host dies, the god goes into the paper. Always. That's what the paper is. Everything that was ever unfinished, underneath. And yours is the most unfinished thing I've ever seen. When you go, it all goes down. All at once. Into the town. Every line of it." Her voice was very steady. "It'll take Lowmarrow, Wren. Every soul in this square. I've seen a village go. I'm not guessing."

She held out her hand.

"Let me Still you," she said. "Now. Tonight. Before. Let me make it quiet, so that when it goes, it goes quietly. I'm not asking because I want to. I'm asking because I won't stand in this square and watch it be rubbed out." She looked at the people of Lowmarrow, faint in the snow. "Please."

Wren looked at her.

"I need one more hour," Wren said.

"For what?"

Wren looked up at the Lamp.

Severine followed the look. Up the black iron post. To the empty glass cage at the top.

"No," she said quietly. "I'm sorry. No."

She raised her hands.

It came out of her like a breath let go after fifty years.

Wren had never seen anything like it. It wasn't drawn. That was the horror of it. Everything in the world was drawn, every person and every god and every stone; even the Fade was drawn, only faintly. It was the guardian. The size of a child of five. It walked out in front of Wren, between Wren and the Stillness, on its small clean pencil legs. It looked up at the white shape towering over it, with its crosshair face.

Just one. On the blank paper where the cobbles had been, with one small pencil finger. A line, and then another, and then a curve. The edge of a cobblestone. And then another. Wren stood on it.

*She always attacks from the left,* said the guardian, not looking round. *Her whole life. Fifty years. I can see it.*

It looked up at Severine, across the square, with its crosshair face.

*I notice everything,* it said.

It was the hardest fight of Wren's life, and the last.

The Stillness erased, and the guardian drew. It took away the cobbles and the guardian sketched them back. It took away the wall of the Crown and Anchor and the guardian sketched a wall, crooked, in pencil, between Severine and Wren. It swept a white arm across the square, and the guardian put a door in the air, Wren's old door, the Courier's door, in grey, and Wren stepped through it and came out behind.

And they came. One by one, out of the paper, the guardian drew them up. The Hound, in pencil, all scruff and construction lines and no colour, barking. And the Veiled, which Wren still carried, out of the corner of the eye, a shape that was only there when the Stillness wasn't looking. The Stillness touched Wren's arm once, and Unwatched went out of them like a candle. It touched the guardian, and the guardian's left arm went blank to the shoulder, and it didn't stop drawing. It just drew with the other hand.

Wren saw it, between blows, out of the corner of their eye: Mira at the edge of the square, white-faced, and the Mender on its knees in the snow in front of her. In the end it was the guardian.

The Stillness raised both its white hands over the Lamp: to erase it. The post, the cage, the steps, all of it. Wren saw it, and saw they couldn't reach it. The white hand went through the door.

And came out the other side, above Severine.

It touched her. Just once. On the shoulder. Her own god's own hand.

And she stopped.

---

The Stillness didn't break. It didn't come apart. It simply stopped, in the middle of the square, with its white hands lowered. And then, slowly, smoothly, the way it did everything, it sat down beside Severine on the half-sewn cobbles. Small. Folded. Like a dog at a fireside.

Severine was on her knees.

She wasn't hurt. She looked at her own shoulder, where her god had touched her, and then she looked at her god, sitting beside her in the snow. She looked at it for a long time. Wren didn't think she had looked at it in fifty years.

"You'd rather it hurt," Severine said, quietly, from the ground. She didn't sound angry. She sounded as though she genuinely wanted to know. "Don't you? All of it. The whole time. You'd rather it *hurt*."

Wren thought about it. Wren gave the question their whole attention.

"I'd rather be there for it," said Wren.

Severine didn't say anything to that.

She put out her hand, slowly, and laid it on the white shape beside her. It didn't feel like anything; Wren could see that from her face. There was nothing there to feel. She left her hand there anyway.

"When I was twelve," Severine said, after a while, to nobody, "before I went to the old woman, I drew it. My god. In my copybook. I thought I'd want to remember what it looked like." She was looking at the white shape under her hand. "I kept the drawing. It's on the wall in my study. Over the desk. It's faded, now. I can't see what it was any more." She was quiet. "I think it was a hare."

### Chapter 28: The Steps

The square was quiet. The snow was coming down slowly, in big soft flakes, the kind that take a long time to fall. The lanterns strung across it were still unlit. The Midwinter Lamp stood at the centre on its black iron post with its seven iron steps, and its glass cage at the top was empty.

The ceremonial taper was lying in the snow at the foot of the steps. Someone had brought it out from the town hall for the lighting, the way they did every year, and nobody had used it for fifteen years.

Oren picked it up.

He held it for a moment. Turned it over, the long wooden pole with the brass cup at the top. The Hound sat at his heel, quiet for once, its lantern-heart burning gold. Oren took a match from his coat, and struck it, and lit the oil in the brass cup. A small blue flame. Then he held the taper out to Wren.

"Nobody's lit it in years," he said.

Wren looked at the flame. Then at the steps. The same steps. Seven of them. Black iron, worn in the middle, with snow on them.

Beside Wren, at knee height, the guardian took a half-step back.

*Everyone's watching,* it said. Very small.

They were.

The whole square had turned. All of Lowmarrow: Mrs Penhallow from the bakery, and the Abney boys, and the old men from the Crown and Anchor, faint in the snow, and their grey gods. Mira's father in the gate of the works. Ada Tolley, who had come down from Cobble Lane with her empty bowl, and stood at the edge of the square in the snow with her folded god. Forty of the Order in white, motionless. Severine on her knees, with her hand on the Stillness. All of them looking at Wren.

*They'll see,* said the guardian. *If you fall. They'll see. They'll laugh.*

Its voice was shaking. It had never once sounded like this, in fifteen years. It sounded like what it was. Something very small, looking up at something very big, in front of a great many people.

*We said,* it whispered. *We said we wouldn't do this again. We said.*

Wren took the taper.

Mira was at the foot of the steps. She didn't come any closer. Wren saw her understand, without being told, that this wasn't hers to carry. She untied the end of the red thread from her own wrist, slowly, with cold fingers. She knelt, and tied it to the iron rail at the foot of the steps in a neat, tight knot, and pulled it twice.

### Chapter 29: Spring

> ◆ **GAME** · **EPILOGUE (playable).** Spring in Lowmarrow, as **Mira**. Short, about ten minutes, walking only. Morning at the works, Wren's room, the evening square. The circle of colour around the square is **visibly larger** than at Midwinter. It has reached the dye works, Cobble Lane and the bridge, and it is still growing.

The blue took.

It took on the Tuesday after Midwinter, and on the Tuesday after that, and by spring it was taking every day. The bolts came out of the blue vat at the Callow Dye Works the colour of the sky above the moor in March, a real blue, deep and wet and true, and they dried on the line in the yard and stayed blue. Old Callow stood in front of them every morning and cried and blamed the steam. Nobody argued with him.

The colour had come down from the square. It had come slowly, all winter, a street at a time, the way the Fade had once gone the other way. By the spring it was in the dye works yard and on Cobble Lane and halfway across the bridge, and every week a little more. Nobody knew how far it would go. Nobody was in any hurry to find out.

Mira was at the window of the vat room.

She was thinner. She looked like someone who has spent a winter grieving and is still doing it; there was no hurrying that, and she didn't try. The Mender was beside her, at the long table, mending the Midwinter banners for next year. They'd been in the town hall loft for fifteen years and were full of holes. It was working slowly. Every stitch was small and careful and exactly where it needed to be. It didn't hurry any more.

Mira set a cup down on the windowsill. Milk, no sugar.

She looked at it for a while.

Then she picked it up and drank it herself, before it went cold.

---

At the dye works gate, the knot was still on the post.

There was no thread in it. The thread was round the iron rail at the foot of the Midwinter Lamp, where Mira had tied it; she had never untied it, and nobody else would. But the first knot, the one the Mender had tied the night Wren left, was still on the gatepost. The rain loosened it sometimes. Whenever it did, the Mender came out and tied it again.

---

She went up to Wren's room in the afternoon. She'd been going once a week, to dust.

The box was still under the bed. She'd never opened it. She opened it now: the white sash, folded small, from the novitiate, which she put aside. And under it, a copybook. A child's school copybook, the cardboard cover soft with age, full of drawings. Birds, mostly. Dozens and dozens of birds, in a child's careful pencil. Wrens. Robins. A heron that was probably somebody's god.

The last page was a drawing of the Midwinter Lamp.

A child on the steps of it, holding up a taper, very proud. A little bird on the child's shoulder, round, with its tail stuck up. And underneath, in a nine-year-old's best careful handwriting, as if it were a promise:

*Me lighting the lamp. And next year again.*

Mira sat on the edge of the bed for a long time with the copybook on her knees.

Then she looked up, at the wall.

The marks were still there. Thousands of them, from the skirting board up. She had never known what they were. Wren had never said. She put her hand flat against them, the way she'd put her hand on the red thread at the gate every morning for eight months, and it was only a wall, and only cold. She didn't count them. She thought perhaps she'd count them one day. Not yet.

She took the copybook with her when she went.

---

In the evening, Oren went round the lamps.

He did it every evening now, with his long pole on his shoulder, the way he'd done it for thirty years before. The Hound bounded ahead of him down every street, barking at a fox that was, for once, actually there. Oren was tired. He was always tired. The Hound woke him four times a night. He stopped at a crooked lamp on the corner of Mill Street, and looked at it, and straightened it, and moved on.

Mira was on the step of the dye works when he came by. He stopped. The Hound sat down at her feet without being asked, and put its head on her knee, and its lantern-heart glowed.

Oren looked at her for a moment, with that big worried face. And then he asked her the question that she'd once asked, every morning, at the gate.

"Is it bad today?"

Mira thought about it. It was an honest question, and she gave it an honest amount of thought.

"Yes," she said.

Oren nodded.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't try to fix it. He sat down on the step beside her, with the Hound between them, and they sat there together while the light went, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

"Cup's on the sill," said Mira eventually, "if you want one."

He did.

---

On his way home across the square, much later, with the stars out and the Midwinter Lamp burning on its post over the colour it had made, Oren passed the house on the corner, where the Pryor girl lived with her husband. She'd had her first baby at the end of the winter.

She was sitting on her doorstep in the lamplight with the baby in her arms. And the baby's god was crawling over the step beside them, brand new, the size of a kitten. It was shrieking at a moth.

It was pencil. Construction lines, grey and faint and quick. A sketch somebody had only just started. You couldn't tell yet what it was going to be.

The girl looked up at Oren with her tired, frightened, brand-new face.

"She's so loud," she said. "Is it — is that normal? Is that all right?"

Oren looked at the little pencil god, shrieking at the moth. Then he looked at the Hound, which was barking at the same moth.

"Oh, it's awful," said Oren.

He reached up with his pole and lit the lamp over her door.

"You'll be all right," he said.

He went on across the square, toward home, with the Hound bounding ahead of him barking at everything. The lamp burned behind him in the snowless spring dark, and the little pencil god on the doorstep looked up at it, and stopped shrieking, and watched it.
