# THE UNFINISHED

## PROLOGUE: LOWMARROW

---

### Chapter 1: Morning

The clock in Wren's room was cheap, and it ticked as if it had been paid by the tick.

Wren had been awake for some time before it reached six. They lay on their side facing the wall, the blanket pulled up to the ear, and watched the grey come into the room the way damp comes into plaster: not arriving, just gradually being there. The ceiling was a pale wash. The wardrobe was three lines and a smudge. In Lowmarrow everything had been drawn a little thinner every year for as long as Wren could remember, and the bedroom had gone the way of the rest of the town, so that some mornings it was hard to tell the furniture from its own shadow.

The wall Wren faced was covered in marks.

They were pencil, very faint, and they ran from the skirting board up to about the height a person could reach lying down. Short upright strokes in groups of four with a fifth struck across, the way prisoners count days in stories. Thousands of them. They went on under the bed. Wren had never seen one being made. They had simply always been there and always been more, the way the gap between two floorboards is always slightly wider than you remember.

Wren had decided a long time ago that it was a ledger. Something keeping score. They had stopped reading it the way you stop opening letters from a creditor.

In the corner of the room, where the wall met the ceiling, a line began to draw itself. A curve, very light, and then another curve beside it, the start of something tall. It didn't finish. It never finished, in the mornings.

*Nobody will notice if you don't get up.*

The voice was Wren's own, older and flatter, and it came from slightly behind Wren's left shoulder, which was impossible, because Wren's left shoulder was against the mattress.

*You know that. The vats will get stirred. Callow will dock the morning and won't even be cross about it. It'll be a relief, honestly. One day off from everyone having to pretend you're worth the wage.*

Wren lay still.

There was no arguing with it. Wren had tried, at eleven and at fourteen and at seventeen, and it was like arguing with a set of accounts. Everything it said was true. That was the difficulty. Callow *would* dock the morning. The vats *would* get stirred. Wren had never caught the voice in a lie about a fact. It was only ever wrong about what the facts were for.

> ◆ **GAME** · Open on black with the clock. Fade in. The player has no control: the stick does nothing. A small pencil prompt fades in bottom-right: **[ GET UP ]**. The first press does nothing. Each further press stirs Wren slightly (eyes open, hand moves). Holding the button raises Wren slowly, over **eleven real seconds**. It should feel heavy, not punishing. When Wren is upright, a single new tally mark draws itself on the wall. Neither the game nor the book ever explains the marks.

Wren's eyes opened. That was the first part. Then the hand, finding the edge of the blanket, which was another part. Wren had learned to think of mornings as a set of parts, like a drawing: you didn't draw a person, you drew a line and then the next line, and if you were lucky, at some point it turned out to have been a person all along.

Wren sat up.

The cold came in at the back of the neck. The voice said nothing, the way a person says nothing who has already said it and is now waiting for you to notice they were right.

On the wall, low down, near the skirting board, one more pencil stroke drew itself. Wren didn't see it. They were looking at the window.

The house opposite had a poster pasted to its gable end. It hadn't been there yesterday. It was printed on good white paper, the only white thing in the street, and it said:

**THE ORDER OF THE STILL HAND**
**RECKONING: TODAY, TOWN HALL**
**ALL WELCOME · NO CHARGE · NO SHAME**

Someone had drawn a small smiling sun in the bottom corner. Wren looked at the sun for a while.

"Morning," Wren said, to the room.

*You're thinking about the Reckoning.*

"I'm thinking about breakfast."

*You're thinking about walking down there and standing in the line. In front of everyone. Seven years later. So the assessor can open his ledger and read it out in front of the whole town: Lowmarrow Subject Eleven, still here, still the one thing in this town the Order couldn't mend.*

Wren found their boots under the bed. The left one had a split at the toe that let in the wet. Wren had been meaning to see to it for a year.

*We won't do that again.*

Wren laced the left boot, then the right, and pulled the laces tight.

*Remember?*

Wren didn't remember. That was the strange thing. The sentence felt like a scar you'd had so long you'd forgotten the cut, and every time the voice said it Wren felt the shape of something underneath and couldn't find the edges. It had been saying it for fifteen years. *We won't do that again.* Do what? Wren had asked, at first. The voice had never answered. After a while Wren had stopped asking, the way you stop asking a dog what it's barking at.

Wren stood up. Wren got dressed. Wren went out.

### Chapter 2: The Works

Lowmarrow in the early morning was a pencil sketch someone had started to ink and then lost interest in.

The main street still had its lines: the cobbles were drawn in, every one, though thinly, and the shopfronts kept their lettering. But the colour had mostly gone out of it during Wren's lifetime, and what was left was the colour of things that have been washed too often. The baker's awning had once been red. Wren could remember it red. It was the pink of a scar now. The great iron lamp-post stood in the middle of the square with its glass cage at the top and rust drawn on it in a few tired hatches. It was lit every night and made very little difference.

Everyone had their god with them. Everyone always did.

Mrs Penhallow at the bakery had a fat speckled hen made of soft grey crayon that sat on the counter and counted the loaves under its breath and lost count and started again. The Abney boys, eight and six, were chasing their gods up the alley, two scribbles the size of kittens, one blue and one a smudgy green, that kept darting ahead and getting called back and darting ahead again. Outside the Crown and Anchor two old men were arguing about a horse, and their gods were arguing about the same horse in the same posture, a pair of gaunt herons with their necks stuck out.

People said hello to Wren. Wren said hello back.

"Morning, Mrs Penhallow."

"Wren, love. Bit of a chill."

"There's always a chill. It's a feature of the town. We should put it on the sign."

Mrs Penhallow laughed. Her hen stopped counting to look at Wren, and lost its place, and started again.

"Morning, Mr Abney. Your boys are up the Fold again."

"Are they? Little — *Tobias!*"

"I didn't see anything. I'm a stranger here."

> ◆ **GAME** · Free-roam walk through Lowmarrow to the dye works: movement and camera tutorial, talking to NPCs. Every NPC has a god beside them, with an idle animation that echoes its host's posture. Wren's dialogue is quick and charming and always ends the conversation first. The player should notice how good Wren is at this. Wren has no god visible beside them.

It was easy. It had always been easy. You said one thing that made someone laugh, and by the time they'd finished laughing you were gone, and they'd remember you as cheerful. The trick was never to stay for the second sentence. The second sentence was where people asked you things.

Wren's mother lived at the top of Cobble Lane, in the house Wren had grown up in, and Wren went past it every morning on the way to the works because there was no other way to go. She was on the doorstep, shelling peas into a bowl, the way she was most mornings now. Her god sat on the step beside her. It had been a heron once, tall and grey and anxious and always about to fly off, and Wren could remember it at night in the kitchen when they were small, pacing, while their mother cried with her back to the room. It was folded now. It sat on the step like a closed umbrella, and never moved.

"Morning, love," said Ada Tolley. "You look tired."

There wasn't any worry in it. It was an observation, the way she might say it looked like rain.

"I'm always tired. It's my face."

"It is a tired sort of face," she agreed pleasantly, and split another pod.

Wren had been nine when two Stillers came to the house and took her to the town hall, and she came back quiet. Wren remembered the evening exactly: the door, and their mother coming through it and taking off her coat and hanging it on the hook, the right way up, which she never did, and then making supper. The heron had walked in behind her, small and grey and silent. That night there was no crying through the wall. Nor the night after. Wren had lain in bed for a week listening to the quiet and feeling something that took years to name, and when they finally named it, it was *relief*, and it was the worst thing Wren knew about themself.

Wren had never known who sent for the Stillers. Nobody had ever said. Wren had never asked, and when the question came near, something in them stepped quickly out of its way, the way you step round a loose board in a floor you've lived on all your life.

She had been kind ever since. She had never been unkind before, either. She had only been there, and now she wasn't, and nobody in Lowmarrow thought of it as a loss, because she slept well and she was pleasant at church and the peas got shelled.

"I'll come Sunday," Wren said.

"That'll be nice."

*She doesn't mind either way. You know that. You could come Sunday or never come again, and she'd say "that'll be nice."*

Wren went on down the lane.

---

The Callow Dye Works stood at the bottom of the town where the river ran slowest. Once there'd been six vats and forty hands and a smell you could taste in the next village. Now there were three vats lit, eleven hands, and a smell that had gone thin like everything else. Wren's job was the blue vat. You stirred it with a paddle taller than you were, and lifted the bolts of cloth out with a hook, and hung them on the line, and watched them dry two shades paler than they'd been in the dye. Every year the dye took a little less. Nobody knew why. The Order said it was the Fade, and the Fade was the world getting old, and nothing could be done about the world getting old.

Mira was at the gate.

She had the ledger under one arm and her hair tied back with a bit of string, and she was waiting, the way she did. Her god was beside her, working at a frayed rope on the gatepost: a long, patient figure made of darning wool and thread, with a needle where its right forefinger should be. The Mender. It was always fixing something. Mira said once that it would darn the sky if it could reach.

"You're late," said Mira.

"I'm early for tomorrow."

She didn't smile. Then she did, a little, against her will, which was how Mira's smiles usually went. She held out a cup.

"Milk, no sugar. Drink it before it goes cold, for once in your life."

Wren took the cup. It was warm through the clay. For two years now, every morning, Mira had left a cup on the windowsill of the vat room, and every morning Wren had got caught up with the paddle and the hook and the line and forgotten it was there, and every evening Mira had tipped it out cold into the yard drain and never said a word. Wren didn't know why she kept doing it. Wren had never asked, because asking would have been the second sentence.

> ◆ **GAME** · Mira hands over the cup. The player can **Drink** or **Hold**. Either way the camera lingers on Wren's hands around it for a beat too long. If they drink, Wren's line later is unchanged. The game only remembers it for one thing: a small variant in Mira's first letter in Chapter 8 ("You drank it. I nearly fell over.").

"I was about to come and knock," Mira said. "Another five minutes and I'd have had my coat on."

Wren drank the tea. It was strong, the way she made it, and a bit too hot.

"You look tired," Mira said.

"Everybody says that. I'm starting to think it's personal."

"Mm." She looked past Wren. Not at Wren: past, just over the left shoulder, at the empty air there, the way people look at a god. People in Lowmarrow had done it all Wren's life. They couldn't see anything, because there was nothing there to see, mostly. But they looked anyway, the way you look at a door you've heard a noise behind.

"Is it bad today?" Mira said.

Nobody else had ever asked that. Not in those words. Not as if the answer might be yes, and that would be all right.

> ◆ **GAME** · First dialogue wheel. Three options, all written in the guardian's pencil: **"I'm fine." · "Just tired." · "Don't worry about me."** There is no honest option and no fourth slot. The wheel itself teaches the player that the guardian writes Wren's answers. (The first honest option appears in the letters, Chapter 8.)

"Just tired," said Wren.

*She asks because she's kind,* said the voice, just behind the ear. *She'd ask the goat. Don't mistake it for anything.*

Mira nodded, as though she'd heard that answer many times before, which she had.

"All right," she said. "The cup'll be on the sill."

She went into the counting house. The Mender finished the rope on the gatepost, looked at its work, and pulled one stitch out and did it again, better. Then it followed her in.

---

The vat room was hot and grey and smelled of wet wool. Wren stirred. Wren lifted. Wren hung the bolts on the line and watched them fade. At eleven o'clock old Callow came through, stooped and red-eyed, and looked at the morning's bolts the way a man looks at a field after hail, and said, "Well," and went out again. At half past eleven the cup on the windowsill had gone cold. Wren noticed it at a quarter to twelve.

At twelve the bell at the town hall began to ring for the Reckoning.

---

Oren Tamsin was at the foot of the square lamp with a ladder, checking the iron.

He was a big man, wide and slow and soft-spoken, with a lamplighter's long pole slung on his back even in daylight out of habit, and a face that looked worried even when it wasn't, because it so often was. His god was circling the ladder. The Lantern-Hound was the size of a pony, all scruff and orange ink, the only properly warm colour on the square, with a lantern burning in its chest where its heart should be. It barked at a cat. It barked at the wind. It barked at a leaf that moved and then at the same leaf when it stopped moving.

"Morning, Wren," said Oren. "You're about early."

"Couldn't sleep."

"Ha. Join the club." He jerked his chin at the Hound. "He had me up checking the shutters four times last night. *Four.* Thought he heard foxes."

"Were there foxes?"

"There's always foxes, according to him." Oren came down a rung, and wiped his hands on his coat, and looked at Wren with that soft, worried face. "You going down to the Reckoning?"

"Me? No. Just walking."

"Mm." He didn't say anything for a moment. The Hound barked at a pigeon. "No shame in it, if you were. My sister went, back in '32. It helped her. I think it helped her." He frowned at nothing. "She sleeps."

The Hound stopped barking.

It turned its great scruffy head and looked at Wren, or not quite at Wren, at the air just behind Wren's left shoulder, and its lantern-heart flared up bright, and the hackles rose along its spine like a brush being dragged the wrong way.

"Hey," said Oren. "Hey. Easy. It's only Wren."

The Hound didn't move. It stared at the empty air, and its lips came back from teeth drawn in thick orange strokes, and it made a sound so low Wren felt it in the cobbles.

"Sorry," said Oren, embarrassed, catching it by the scruff. "Sorry. He's — it's the Reckoning, all the gods are jumpy today, the Order makes them —"

"It's fine," said Wren.

*He knows,* said the voice, pleased. *Even the dog knows.*

Wren walked on across the square, past the town hall, where the line was already out of the door and down the steps, past the white canopy and the two white figures standing at the door with their hands folded, past all of it, and on down Mill Street toward the river and home.

> ◆ **GAME** · Hard cut. No transition, no fade. Wren is standing in the Reckoning line inside the town hall. The player never controls the decision to join it. One moment they're walking away; the next, they're in line. The decision happened somewhere the player wasn't allowed to see.

### Chapter 3: The Reckoning

The town hall smelled of floor polish and nerves.

The line wound twice around the room between ropes. Up at the front, under a white canopy set up where the mayor's table usually stood, an assessor of the Order sat at a small desk with a ledger open in front of him. He was young and neat and drawn in very clean ink, much cleaner than anything in Lowmarrow, so that he looked slightly pasted-in, like a figure from a newer book. Two Stillers stood behind him in white. They didn't move. They hadn't moved, as far as Wren could tell, since the doors opened.

Wren was eleventh from the front, and could not remember deciding to be.

One by one the line went up. The assessor smiled at each person, asked their name, wrote in his ledger, and then reached out two fingers and rested them, very gently, on the forehead of the person's god. The gods went quiet under his fingers. Most of them, after a moment, were let go. The assessor would murmur something, and write something, and hand over a little white pamphlet, and the person would go out the side door looking a little relieved and a little foolish, the way people look coming out of a doctor's having been told it's nothing.

Some of them were taken behind the curtain.

A young man went behind it while Wren was fourth from the front. Bram Hollis, from the tannery. His god was a sort of furious terrier made of scribbled red, which bit at his heels and at other people's and was always being apologized for. Bram's mother had brought him. She stood by the curtain twisting her hands.

He came out after a few minutes. The terrier walked behind him, smaller, its red gone grey, and quiet. Bram looked around the hall with an expression Wren had seen before, on a doorstep at the top of Cobble Lane: mild, interested, rested. His mother took one look at him and began to cry, and Bram patted her shoulder kindly and didn't seem to understand why she was crying, and they went out together.

*Look at him,* said the voice, very close. *Look how calm he is. That could be you.*

Wren looked at him.

*All you'd have to do is let them try again. And when it doesn't work, which it won't, because it didn't last time, everyone in this room gets to watch them write it down. Lowmarrow Subject Eleven, unbindable. Everyone in this room gets to know that you're the thing that couldn't be fixed.*

Wren reached the front.

"Name?" said the assessor pleasantly.

"Wren. Tolley. Of Callow's." Then, because it was going to come out anyway: "I was in the novitiate. A while ago."

The assessor ran his finger down the ledger, and turned back several pages, and then a great many pages, and stopped. His finger rested on a line. He looked up at Wren with an expression that had not been on his face for any of the ten people before. Recognition. And under it, something else. *Interest.*

"Lowmarrow Subject Eleven," he said.

He didn't say it loudly. He didn't have to. The hall was quiet, and people were standing close. Wren heard a shuffle as heads turned in the line behind.

"Would you show me your god, please?"

"It doesn't — it doesn't really come out," said Wren. Their voice did something they hadn't told it to, a small catch in the middle of *really*.

"Then I'll go to it," said the assessor, kindly, and stood, and leaned over the desk, and reached two fingers toward the empty air behind Wren's left shoulder.

*No,* said the voice. It wasn't flat any more. *No. Not this line. Not this room. We said. We said we wouldn't do this again.*

> ◆ **GAME** · Slow-motion beat as the fingers approach. The audio thins to the scratch of a pencil on paper, getting louder.

The world showed its workings.

That was the only way Wren could ever describe it afterward. Every line in the hall drew itself at once: not the lines of things, but the lines *under* things, the ones that had been there first. Perspective lines shot across the floorboards to vanishing points in the corners. A grid drew itself over the walls in pale blue pencil, squares on squares. Circles appeared around every head in the room, the circles an artist sketches to place a skull before drawing a face over it, and crosshairs inside them. Guidelines ran from every elbow to every hand. The room was suddenly a page in a sketchbook, its scaffolding showing, and everyone in it was a construction drawing of themself.

The assessor's hand went into a pencil circle in the air and came out without the fingers.

He didn't bleed. There was nothing to bleed. The hand simply ended at the knuckles in a soft rubbed-out edge, like a mistake taken out with an eraser. He looked at it. He began to scream.

Behind Wren, something stood up.

Wren didn't turn. Wren didn't need to. They could see it in the faces of the people in the line: every face turned up, and up, and still up. Lines were folding together behind Wren, construction lines on construction lines, gathering into a shape twice the height of a person, long-armed, faceless, made entirely of the faint grey under-marks of a drawing that hasn't been inked. Where its face would have been there was only an oval, and a crosshair in it, the mark an artist makes to say *the eyes go here, the nose here*, with nothing ever put there.

It turned its crosshair slowly across the room. Across all the faces looking at Wren.

*Don't look at us,* it said. And this time it wasn't only Wren who heard it.

> ◆ **GAME** · **ESCAPE: THE TOWN HALL.** First action sequence. The guardian redraws the hall around Wren in real time: the floor tilts forty degrees, ceiling beams draw themselves down as bars across the aisle, a doorway is rubbed out just as Wren reaches it, a staircase is drawn upward into nothing. The player learns sprint, vault and dodge under pressure. The guardian never chases. It *erases the distance*, and appears closer each time the camera turns. Townsfolk scatter as panicking construction drawings. Exit: the side door, which the guardian forgets to erase.

Wren ran.

The floor tipped under them like a deck. Wren went down on one hand and up again, and a beam came down out of the ceiling in a long grey stroke across the aisle ahead and Wren went under it. People were screaming, and some of them were screaming at the sight of each other, at the circles round their heads, at the pencil grids on their skin. A door was ahead and then it wasn't. It went out like a blown candle, a doorframe with nothing in it and then a wall. Wren turned. A staircase was drawing itself up the wall to the left, step by step, and Wren's feet were on it before Wren knew they'd chosen it, and it went up and up and stopped in midair. Wren dropped off the side of it and hit the floor and rolled, and there, at the far end of the hall, was the side door, the one Bram Hollis had gone out of with his quiet terrier, and it was still a door. Wren hit it with a shoulder.

The square was full of daylight.

It should have helped. It didn't. Wren was halfway across the cobbles when they felt the cold at their back, and heard the town hall door open behind them, and didn't hear it close.

*Stop,* said the guardian. *Stop running. Everyone's watching you run.*

They were. Lowmarrow had come out of every door on the square. Mrs Penhallow at the bakery, with flour on her hands. The Abney boys, holding their scribble-gods tight against their chests. Old men outside the Crown and Anchor. Their gods had all gone very small and very still.

*Do you know what you look like?*

Wren looked back.

It was coming across the square, and it wasn't walking, exactly. It was simply being drawn a little nearer each moment, the way a draughtsman redraws a figure in the next panel, a step closer, a step closer, without the step in between. The cobbles went to pencil grid under it. The colour, what little there was, went out of everything within ten feet of it.

It was a yard away. It was a foot away. The crosshair of its face was turned down at Wren, and its long arms came up, and Wren could see, all the way along them, the faint pencil guidelines that hadn't been rubbed out: little marks along the forearm that said *wrist here* and *hand here* and *grip*.

Something hit it from the side like a falling house.

### Chapter 4: The Hound

The Lantern-Hound had its jaws on the guardian's arm.

It had come across the square from the lamp-post in three bounds. Wren had seen the last one from the corner of an eye: an orange blur, the lantern in its chest flaring white-hot, all four feet off the ground. It had hit the guardian at the shoulder and borne it sideways, and now it was standing over Wren with its legs spread and its teeth sunk in pencil, shaking its head the way a dog shakes a rat. Orange ink splashed off its jaws onto the cobbles and stayed there, bright as paint.

Across the square Oren was running, his lamplighter's pole bouncing on his back, his face white.

"*Lamp!*" Oren was shouting. It was the Hound's name. Wren had never heard it used before. "Lamp, *leave* it — *Lamp!*"

The guardian looked down at the Hound.

It didn't pull away. It didn't seem hurt. It held quite still, with the Hound's teeth in its forearm, and turned its crosshair down to look at the thing biting it, with what Wren would have sworn, if it had had a face, was curiosity.

*Oh,* it said. *You're a worrier.*

It put its free hand on the Hound's head.

The construction lines ran out of its fingers. Wren watched them go: fine grey guide-marks threading down through the Hound's orange scruff, along its spine, into its legs, down into the lantern in its chest. Under each one, the orange went faintly grey. The Hound's eyes, which were two warm daubs of brown, went white, like the whites of an unfinished drawing.

Its jaws opened. It let go.

It turned around, very slowly, to face Wren.

*He knows,* said the guardian, gently. *He's always known. He's been waiting thirty years for the thing in the dark, and checking the shutters, and checking them again. And here it is.*

The Hound's lips came back. The lantern in its chest burned white.

*Here you are.*

> ◆ **BOSS: THE LANTERN-HOUND (tutorial)** · Big, fast, readable. **Charge** (long wind-up, dodge sideways), **Bite** (short lunge, parry window), **Lantern Burst** (the heart flares, an AoE ring; dodge *through* it). It teaches dodge, light attack and parry. Phase 2 at half health: it *circles*, the way it circled the ladder, and the player has to read which way it's going to break. Oren is at the arena's edge for the whole fight, calling *"Lamp!"* He's not an obstacle and can't be hit. The guardian stands at the edge of the square, watching, and says nothing.

Wren had never fought anything.

Wren had been in the novitiate for two years at fifteen and sixteen, and the Order did not teach fighting; it taught stillness, and hands folded, and how to rest two fingers on the brow of a frightened god. Wren had been bad at all of it. But Wren found, now, that the body had its own opinions, and its first opinion was *sideways*. The Hound came across the cobbles like a dropped cartload of fire and Wren went sideways, and it went past, and its lantern-heat went over Wren's face like an oven door opening.

"Don't hurt him!" Oren was shouting. "Please — Wren, don't — he doesn't mean — *Lamp!*"

It came again. Wren went sideways again. There was a broom against the bakery wall. Wren didn't remember picking it up.

It was the longest minute of Wren's life until then. The Hound circled the way it circled the ladder, around and around, barking at nothing and at Wren and at the white thing in its own chest, and lunged, and missed, and lunged. Wren hit it with the broom-handle across the face. It didn't seem to notice. Wren hit it again, and it yelped, and Wren nearly dropped the broom.

The lantern in its chest flared. Wren saw it coming, the light going white, and some instinct said *not away, through*, and Wren ran at the Hound and went past its shoulder as the ring of heat went out across the square and over Mrs Penhallow's awning and set the edge of it smoking.

Then it was down. Wren wasn't sure how. It was on its side on the cobbles, its great orange flank heaving, its legs scrabbling. The lantern in its chest guttered, white, orange, white. It was trying to get up. It was going to get up.

A prompt appeared in the air before Wren, in faint grey pencil:

**[ TAKE IT ]**

Wren knew, without knowing how, that it was the guardian's handwriting.

> ◆ **GAME** · The only prompt is **[ TAKE IT ]**. There's no alternative. If the player waits, the Hound gets to its feet and the fight resumes at full fury. The player cannot leave. (This sets up Chapter 7, where for the first time the game offers to let them walk away, and then takes that back.)

Wren didn't want to.

Wren was very clear about that afterwards, at three in the morning, on many mornings: they did not want to. But the Hound was getting up, and its eyes were white, and Oren was screaming its name across the square, and the guardian was standing at the edge of everything with its hands folded in front of it like a Stiller, waiting. And Wren's hand was already moving. It went forward, into the orange scruff of the Hound's chest, and closed around the lantern.

It was hot. It was hotter than anything. It didn't burn.

The orange ran up Wren's arm. That was what it felt like: like dye going up a thread. It ran into the wrist and up the forearm under the skin and over the shoulder, warm and bright and *frightened*, all the Hound's thirty years of locks and shutters and noises in the night pouring into Wren at once, and then it was in, and it was quiet, and the Hound came apart.

It didn't die. It wasn't like dying. It unravelled into lines, the way a drawing is unmade if you take each stroke off in the order it went on, and the lines lifted, and were gone.

For one frame, if anyone had been looking, they would have seen that the small gap in Wren's outline at the left shoulder, where the line didn't quite meet, had grown wider. No one was looking.

Across the square, Oren stopped shouting.

He had been running, and he stopped running. He stood there on the cobbles with his arms a little out from his sides, and his face changed. It was like watching a fist open. The lines of worry went out of it, one by one, and the shoulders came down an inch, and then another. He looked around the square as if he'd just walked into it.

He came over to Wren. He came slowly, unhurried, the way people walk in summer.

"Are you hurt?" he said.

"Oren." Wren's voice was not working properly. "Oren, I'm sorry. I didn't — it was going to —"

"It's all right," said Oren.

He tilted his head, the way you do to listen for a sound that has stopped.

"It's very quiet," he said.

He looked up at the sky, which was pale and blank and going to rain, and he smiled a little, and his face was smooth.

"I think this is good," he said.

Behind them, the guardian was gone. The cobbles where it had stood were still faintly gridded, as though someone had begun to draw the square over again and given up. All around the edges of the square, the people of Lowmarrow stood in their doorways looking at Wren. Nobody said anything.

*Look at you,* said the voice, very quietly, for Wren alone. *You couldn't even get out of a line without hurting someone.*

### Chapter 5: The Gate

The assessor's hand had grown back by evening, more or less. A doctor came from Fellbridge and drew it in again for him, and he went about the inn with it held up in front of him like something borrowed, looking at it. He did not go back to the town hall. The Reckoning was suspended. The two Stillers in white walked once around the square in the late afternoon, side by side, and looked at the gridded cobbles, and at the dye works, and at the lane where Wren lived, and then went into the inn and did not come out.

Nobody came to Wren's door. Wren had expected someone to. Wren sat on the bed with their boots still on and waited for the knock, and it didn't come, and that was worse.

At ten, Wren packed. It took nine minutes. There wasn't much: a coat, a second shirt, the boots already on. The box under the bed, with the old white sash and the copybook in it, Wren looked at for a long time and then pushed back under the bed with a foot.

The plan, such as it was, had arrived in the afternoon fully formed, the way the worst ideas do. The Order couldn't Still Wren's god at seventeen. Perhaps they could now. Perhaps the Stillhouse itself, far in the north, where the best of them were, could do what an assessor in a country town hall could not. And if not, at least Wren would be four hundred miles from Lowmarrow when it next stood up, and whatever it did next, it would do to strangers.

*You think leaving town will help,* the voice said at last, conversationally, as Wren laced the coat. *I don't live in Lowmarrow. I live in you.*

"I know," said Wren.

---

Oren was waiting at the end of Cobble Lane, in the dark, with a pack on his back and his lamplighter's pole and a lantern, unlit. He was sitting on the wall, swinging one foot. He looked up as Wren came down the lane and smiled at them pleasantly.

"Off north?" he said.

Wren stopped.

"How did you know?"

"I didn't." He thought about it. "I just thought I'd sit here."

"Oren, you can't come with me."

"No?" He considered this too. "Why not?"

Because I took your god out of your chest with my hand, Wren thought. Because I'm the reason you're sitting on a wall at ten at night not minding anything. Because every time I look at you I'm going to see it.

"Why would you want to?" Wren said instead.

Oren thought about that one for a long time. It was too long. Wren found they were holding their breath.

"I don't really feel strongly," Oren said at last, apologetically, "about staying."

He swung his foot.

Wren didn't have an answer to that. They walked on, and after a moment Oren slid off the wall and fell in beside them, his pole on his back, and they went down the lane toward the works and the river road.

Mira was at the dye works gate.

She was in her coat, buttoned to the throat, with a bag at her feet, and the Mender standing behind her in the dark, its thread-fingers moving slightly, the way they did when she was nervous. She didn't look surprised to see them. She looked like someone who had been standing at a gate for some time, deciding to keep standing there.

"I'm coming," said Mira.

"You're not."

"Father can keep the books for a month. He'll get them wrong. He can still keep them."

"Mira." Wren turned and pointed back at Oren, who was looking up at the stars with mild interest, the lantern hanging from his hand unlit. "Look at him. Look. I did that. I didn't even mean to and I did that. That's what happens to people who stand near me."

*Tell her,* said the voice. *Tell her what you'll do to her. You'll take the Mender out of her the same way, some bad night on some road, and she'll stand there smiling at you, and you'll have to look at it every day for the rest of your life.*

"It wasn't you," said Mira. "I saw it. I was at the window. That thing —" and her eyes went past Wren's shoulder, into the dark there, "— that thing did it."

"That thing is me," said Wren.

It was the first time they had ever said it out loud. It came out quite steadily. It sat there in the cold between them.

Mira had no answer to it. Wren watched her look for one, and not find it, and not pretend to. That was one of the things about Mira.

The Mender had an answer. While they stood there, it reached out past Mira's shoulder, unasked, with its needle-finger, and took hold of the frayed cuff of Wren's coat, which had been coming apart for a year, and began to darn it. Small, neat, quick stitches. Wren looked down at it. Wren didn't pull away.

"Then let me come anyway," Mira said quietly.

"No."

She looked at Wren for a long time. She didn't argue. Mira never argued past the point where it would have turned into begging.

The Mender did something instead. It reached into its own woollen chest, the way a person feels in a pocket, and drew out a spool of thread. It was red. Real red, deep and wet-looking, the colour of the baker's awning when Wren was a child. Wren hadn't seen red like that in years. It wasn't from any shop. Gods' things never were. It was the stuff the Mender was made of, wound small.

It tied the loose end to the dye works gatepost in a neat, tight knot, and pulled it twice to test it. Then it held the spool out to Wren.

Mira looked embarrassed. "It does that," she said. "It can't stand a loose end."

"Mira, I can't take —"

"Humour it." She took the spool from the Mender and pushed it into Wren's coat pocket herself, and kept her hand there over it for a second longer than she needed to. "Keep it in your pocket. That's all. It'll feel better."

She didn't say who would feel better.
