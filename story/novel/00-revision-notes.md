# THE UNFINISHED: Revision Notes

The novel in this folder is the canonical version of the story. Where it disagrees with the treatment (`../01-the-unfinished.md`), the novel wins. The treatment still describes the earlier, redemptive ending and should be read as a historical draft.

This is the **second revision pass**. The brief was: make it genuinely dark, let depression win and show that as a bad thing, cut anything corny, and cut anything that only exists to make a scene land. Every beat in the ending now has to be set up earlier in the book, by something the player has already seen.

---

## How to read the novel as a game script

- **Each chapter is one playable segment** (a level, a hub visit or a cutscene). Chapter order is play order.
- Prose is close third person on Wren, past tense, except Chapters 29–30, which are close third on **Mira**.
- **The guardian's speech is in italics with no quotation marks.** Only Wren can hear it, unless it's fully manifested. Everyone can *see* it; it's a god.
- Gameplay is marked with blocks like this:

> ◆ **GAME** · What the player controls, prompts, mechanics, boss rules, and what the novel compresses.

- The novel follows **one canonical route** through Act 3's choices. Where the game branches, a GAME block says so.

---

## What the story is now

**Wren does everything right, and the guardian wins anyway.**

Wren gives the gods back. Wren tells Mira the truth. Wren goes home, fights the guardian, wins, walks up the lane in full view and tells their mother. Wren starts saying "yes" when asked if it's bad. Then, on an ordinary night after an ordinary bad day, it finishes them. Mira finds them in the morning.

The voice is never proven right. Every claim it made is visibly false by the end: people noticed (sixty of them on the hill); Mira didn't leave; trying wasn't pointless (Ambrose, Hal, Oren and Tess got their lives back, and Tess's letter says so). **It was wrong about everything, and it won anyway.** That's the darkness. Not that the voice was right, but that being wrong didn't stop it.

## Pass 2: what was cut, and what replaced it

### 1. The Midwinter Lamp, the taper, and the child-lighting tradition: cut entirely
**Why:** a town ritual where one child climbs the steps to light the lamp existed only so the ending could have the same child climb the same steps. It was corny and it was invented for the payoff.
**Now:** the lamp in the square is just a lamp. Oren lights it every night. It makes very little difference.

### 2. The origin wound: a fall and a laughing crowd → Wren sent their mother to be Stilled
**Why:** "kid trips, crowd laughs, god turns evil" was thin, and it had nothing to do with the rest of the world.
**Now:** at nine, Wren watched their mother cry at the kitchen sink every night with the tap running and her back to the room. Wren walked alone to a Reckoning at the town hall and asked the Order to help her. They Stilled her that afternoon. That night Wren lay in the quiet and felt *relief*, and the little bird god turned into the guardian. Its first sentence, *We won't do that again*, now has a specific meaning: **we won't tell anyone, we won't ask, look what asking does.**

Everything already in the book now points at this:
- Ada was Stilled (Ch 2), Wren felt relief (Ch 2), and Wren has "never known who sent for the Stillers" (Ch 2).
- In Ch 3 the guardian wrecks the town hall at the moment Wren stands in the same line, at the same desk, again (*"Not this line. Not this room."*).
- The dialogue wheel with no honest option (Ch 2), the letters pre-written in pencil (Ch 8), and Wren never staying for the second sentence (Ch 2) are all the same rule.
- The flashback (Ch 14) is triggered by Juniper crying in the wings with her back to the house, the same posture as Ada at the sink.
- The flashback is the **only time the dialogue wheel shows nothing but Wren's own ink**. All three options are true. Honesty is what did this, as far as a nine-year-old can tell.
- The copybook under the bed ("I used to draw. Before.") ends on a drawing of the kitchen: *Mam is sad. Tomorrow I will tell the town hall.* Every page after it is blank.

### 3. The guardian's redemption: cut
**Before:** the guardian shrinks as gods are returned, softens, catches Wren on the fifth step, fights on Wren's side in the final boss, and becomes a finished little bird.
**Now:** returning the gods pulls the underdrawing in, and it reads as healing. **It isn't.** The underdrawing is withdrawing from the world *into Wren*. Each time the guardian re-forms it is more **finished**: darker lines, then Wren's coat, then the start of Wren's face, and at the end a clean, inked, perfect drawing of Wren. It looks rested. It looks like Ada.

This uses the title and the art style rather than inventing anything. All game long, *finished* has meant *dead*: Ambrose's perfect lifeless bowls ("it looked like a picture of a bowl"), the Stilled drawn in clean closed lines, Severine "drawn in the cleanest lines Wren had ever seen". An unfinished drawing is a living one. The guardian wants to finish Wren, and in the end it does.

Lines that read as softening on a first play read as something else on a second:
- *"They'd have taken you. Before you'd finished."* (Ch 21, when it rubs out a bridge to stop the Stillers.) Wren thanks it.
- *"Then home."* (Ch 24.) It helps Wren over a stile. It only wants Wren to get home.

Its Act 3 voice is **calm and reasonable**, never cruel. That's deliberate, and it's the most frightening thing in the book. *"I'm only saying. It's allowed."*

### 4. The tally marks: no explaining line
**Before:** *"What were you counting?" "Mornings." "Five thousand, four hundred and eighty."* It was a poster line delivered by a redeemed villain.
**Now:** the marks are never explained. Wren sees the guardian draw one at Hollin's Lamp, *"the way a clerk enters a sum in a column he doesn't like the look of"*, and asks what they are. It doesn't answer. The design intent: **they're the guardian's ledger of its losses.** One mark for every morning Wren got up, and in the game one for every time the player died and got up. On the last morning there's no mark. Players who've watched them all game will understand. Players who haven't will still get Mira not counting them: *"She thought that whatever it was, it would be too small."*

### 5. The final boss: Severine and the Stillness → the guardian on Cobble Lane
**Why:** Severine's god being "kept inside her for fifty years" and the hare drawing in her study were introduced in the last act to make the climax work. The "always from the left" door trick was set up and paid off in the same fight.
**Now:** Severine has no god (as in Ch 10), and she never comes to Lowmarrow. She said she'd come if the underdrawing reached a town. It doesn't, so she doesn't. Her last appearance is a letter: *"I do not understand what you have done. I would like to. Come north in the spring."*
The final boss is the guardian, on the lane up to Ada's door, which it redraws as the Chapter 3 Reckoning queue with the whole town in it. Wren has almost nothing. **Unwatched** makes the fight easy, and it also makes the door vanish, because a door you knock on to say something to someone's face only exists while you're seen. The player has to drop it and walk the last forty yards visible. They win.

### 6. Winning the fight doesn't save Wren
Wren tells Ada everything. Ada is Stilled. *"Well. I don't mind, love."* The one person who could forgive Wren is the person Wren's request erased, and there's nobody left to do it. Then the guardian stands at the bottom of the lane with its face finished, and waits.
The music cuts on the knock and never comes back. The game doesn't end.

### 7. A new chapter of ordinary days (Ch 28)
A week of mornings. **[ GET UP ]** takes longer each day: 11, 12, 14, 17, 20, 24 seconds, and the game never comments on it. The dialogue wheel now offers honest options in Wren's ink, and Wren uses them. Wren stays for the second sentence with Mrs Penhallow. Wren says *"No, Mam. Not really."* Wren answers *"Is it bad today?"* with *"Yes,"* for the first time in the book. Mira walks them home and sits with them until they sleep.
**Nobody makes a mistake.** Mira doesn't leave early or miss a sign. Oren is checking Wren's window four times a night. That's the point: it wasn't anyone's fault, and it won anyway.

### 8. The death: the prompt is taken away, not failed
The player is **never made to hold [ GET UP ] and fail.** That would tell a player with lived experience that trying doesn't work. Instead, Chapter 29 opens exactly like Chapter 1, on black with the clock, and **the prompt never appears.** The player has been given 5,000-odd mornings and isn't given this one. Then the game cuts to Mira at the gate.
The death is never shown and has no method. Mira finds Wren **whole**: the outline closed all the way round for the first time since the Stitches, every line perfect, "the most finished thing she had ever seen. It looked like a drawing of a person." The guardian is gone too: *"It had been Wren all along. It had never had anywhere else to go."* Depression doesn't get to live either.

### 9. Mira's playable morning (Ch 29)
Set up in Ch 2 (*"Another five minutes and I'd have had my coat on"*) and paid off at Hollin's Lamp (*"you always came round the corner of Mill Street just before I went to get you"*). The player stands at the gate and decides when to put the coat on. Then they walk the Ch 2 route backwards with a cup going cold in their hand. Running is allowed and changes nothing.

### 10. The epilogue: grief, and the world unchanged
Cut: the colour flooding back, the blue dye taking, Mira drinking the cup, *"Me lighting the lamp. And next year again"*, the baby's pencil god, and Oren's *"You'll be all right."*
Now:
- The Fade goes on. Four people got their lives back, and it made no difference to the colour of anything.
- **Juniper:** when Wren dies, the Veiled goes home. She refused it and said *please*, and it went home anyway, on the evening Wren died. The death hurts even the person who said no. This was set up in Ch 20: gods want to go home.
- **Tess** writes to Wren, care of the dye works: Nell is alive, not well, and Tess is staying. *"I wanted you to know you did that."* Mira can't send it on.
- **Severine** writes that she'll pray Wren is at peace. Mira burns it: *"She'd seen what peace looked like. It was forty glass bowls on a scrubbed floor."* This is the one line in the book that rejects "at peace" outright, and it does so with an image from inside the story.
- **The Reckoning comes back** in March. The line is longer. The assessor's hand is fine.
- **Mira stops putting the cup out** in April. She doesn't decide to.
- The last exchange is Oren asking Mira *"Is it bad today?"* and Mira answering *"Yes."* Someone asks and someone answers honestly, which is the thing the guardian spent fifteen years forbidding. Then Oren goes on round the lamps, because it's dark and that's his job.

### 11. Smaller consistency fixes
- **The red thread:** no more unexplained infinite spool. It's the Mender's own substance, pulled out of its chest, so it pays out as long as Mira has it to give. In Act 3 it thins to the pink of the faded awning and runs out, because Mira does.
- **The thread at the gate:** *"Then you'll be at the end of the lane, and you'll know which way's home"* was cut. The Mender ties it on unasked, and Mira is embarrassed: *"It does that. It can't stand a loose end."*
- **Tess's letter:** Nell no longer "married a cooper and has a spare room." It says *"I'm not better. You said once you'd always come. Where are you,"* dated two years back. Stilled Tess reads it and says *"That's a shame."*
- **Mira's letters:** dropped the rule that every letter ends with *"There's a cup on the windowsill."* Repeated fourteen times, it was a jingle. The aphorism *"I'm not keeping the cup for whoever comes back fixed"* was cut. Her last letter now says *"I love you"* plainly, says it looks ridiculous written down, and says she'll come north if she doesn't hear. Wren reads it aloud in the Quiet and says *"That's nice."* That letter is why she's at the gate in Ch 19.
- **Ada's age at Stilling:** Wren was nine, not eleven, so the voice's "fifteen years" and the flashback agree.
- **Cut lines:** *"I do ask the goat. I don't ask the goat how it takes its tea."* *"You fidget."* *"I'd rather be there for it."* *"I'd be frightened too."* *"I know what that looks like."* The last two were guardian sympathy beats, which no longer fit.

## Kept, because it works and it's earned

Oren's *"I think this is good"* and *"Oh, it's awful… Thank you."* Ambrose's *"Look how many"* and *"they're awful."* Juniper's *"What do you think the sash is?"* and her refusal. Hal's names. The midpoint, where Wren pins their own guardian with the five gods while the Stillers erase it. The Quiet, with its 15–20 minutes of silence. *"I don't know how I'd know."* Saying it out loud at Hollin's Lamp. *"Is this not bad?"* Mira unpicking the stitches: *"Then I'll just be here." "That's not much." "It's what I've got."*

## Safety: how a story where depression wins stays on the right side

These are the README rules applied to this ending. They're not optional, and the lived-experience read before shipping (Rule 7) matters more for this draft than for any earlier one.

| Rule | How this draft keeps it |
|---|---|
| The voice is never proven right | Every claim is visibly falsified: sixty people on the hill, Mira stayed, four lives restored, Tess's letter. It wins without being right. |
| Death is never release | Wren's body is a horror image: perfect, lifeless, "a drawing of a person", the bowls. The guardian dies too. The aftermath is long and costly: the Hound, Ada at the grave, Juniper, Mira stopping the cup. Severine's "at peace" is burned. |
| No method | Nothing is shown. The death happens off-page, in metaphor (a drawing being finished), between two chapters. |
| Don't teach "trying doesn't work" | The player is never made to try and fail. The prompt is withheld, not refused. Wren's trying works for everyone else, on-screen. |
| Asking for help isn't the villain | The child was right to ask. Mira says so directly: *"That's what you're supposed to do… It was what they had to give."* The Order's erasure is the wrong answer, not the asking. The final exchange is someone asking and someone answering honestly. |
| The Stilled are not villains | Ada, Juniper, Hal, Tess and Severine are written with care. Juniper's choice is respected, and losing it is a tragedy, not a lesson. |
| Content note and resources | Content note at launch; resources line after the credits. |

**The most sensitive moments, for the lived-experience readers:** Ch 22 (*"It's allowed"*), Ch 23 (*"Who's going to decide for you?"*), the end of Ch 28, and Ch 29. If readers find the guardian's calm Act 3 voice persuasive rather than frightening, that's the thing to fix first.
