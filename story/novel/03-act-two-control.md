### Chapter 11: The Stillhouse

They tried on the first morning, in the Chapel of the Folded Hand.

It was the room Wren remembered best from the novitiate and liked least: long, white and high, with no windows and no altar, only a floor of white stone polished until it looked like still water, and rows of white benches facing nothing. The light came from nowhere in particular. Seven Stillers in white stood in a ring on the polished floor. Wren knelt in the middle of the ring, as they had at seventeen, with their hands folded in their lap.

Severine knelt in front of Wren and reached out two fingers toward the air behind Wren's left shoulder.

Nothing happened.

That was what Wren remembered from seventeen as well: the nothing. The fingers went into the air where the god should be and found air. Severine moved them a little to the left. A little higher. She closed her eyes, and her face took on the patient, listening look of someone feeling for a light switch in the dark. The Stillers around the ring began, very softly, to hum: a low single note, the note the Order used to quiet a god before it touched it.

Nothing.

*I'm not there,* said the voice, from somewhere. From everywhere. It seemed to come from behind Wren's left shoulder and from the polished floor and from the white ceiling all at once. *I've never been there. Were they expecting me to sit still and wait for them?*

Severine opened her eyes. She lowered her hand.

"It's like a draught," she said, quietly, almost to herself. "You feel it on your face. And you turn to find the window, and there isn't one." She sat back on her heels and looked at Wren for a long time. She looked at Wren the way Ambrose had looked at a ninth draft: with total attention and a sort of grief.

"But there's something else," she said. "Isn't there?"

She reached out again. Not behind Wren this time. She laid her two fingers very lightly on Wren's chest, over the breastbone, where the warm frightened orange lived, and the paper restlessness, and the clear cold glass.

The Stillers stopped humming.

"*Oh,*" said Severine.

They had tea in the refectory afterwards, at a long white table, just the two of them. Severine poured. She made it the way the Order made everything: precisely, with no fuss and no flourish, and the tea tasted of almost nothing.

"Three," she said. "There are three gods inside you."

"Yes."

"Taken. With your hand. In the field. Without rites, without the hum, without the Fold. And they've gone *quiet*." She shook her head slowly. "Do you know how long it takes a Stiller to learn to quiet one god? With seven brothers and sisters holding the note? Eleven years, on average. And you —" She stopped. "How did it feel?"

Wren thought about the lantern in their fist. The orange running up their arm.

"Like dye," they said. "Going up a thread."

Severine was silent for a while.

"There are gods out there," she said eventually, "doing terrible harm. Not many. A handful a year. Feral beyond anything a Reckoning can reach. Last winter on the coast a man's god drowned the road to Saltby, and three carts with it. In Velle there's a god that has put two stagehands in hospital this season, and the poor woman it belongs to won't come to us. She's frightened of us." Severine turned her cup on its saucer. "We can't reach them, Wren. We're slow, and we need consent, and we need seven of us and a quiet room. You don't."

"You want me to take them."

"I want you to help them. Some of them are in agony. Some of them are going to hurt someone they love." She looked up. "And in return, I'll give you every hour of every day I have left, and the best minds in the Order, until we find a way to quiet *yours*."

*Say yes,* said the voice.

Wren looked up, startled. It never asked for anything.

*Say yes. Think about it. Think how good you'd be.*

"Yes," Wren said.

They gave Wren a room in the east tower. Not the old cell: a proper room, with a window and a desk, and on the bed, folded square, a white sash. It was the same kind Wren had worn at fifteen, and had kept in a box under the bed in Lowmarrow, and had not been able to throw away. Only this one had a thin line of silver along the edge. A Warden's sash.

Wren put it on.

Wren trained. Wren had never been good at anything in the Order before, and now Wren was good at this. There was a yard at the back of the Stillhouse where the Wardens practised, and Wren went there every morning, and the gods inside them woke when Wren called. The Courier's paper restlessness let Wren draw a door in the air and step through it and come out somewhere else. The Draft's clear cold glass found the moment in a strike when everything lined up, and made it perfect. And the Hound's warm orange went out in a pulse and showed Wren everything: every enemy behind every wall, every blow before it came.

They were good. Wren was *good*. The Wardens stopped training when Wren trained, and watched.

And the guardian came back.

It came back in the training yard, at dusk, a week after Wren arrived. It stood at the edge of the white flagstones and watched Wren work. And Wren, for the first time in their life, wasn't afraid to see it, because Wren could see, now, that it had changed.

It had grown. It was taller. And it was wearing things.

On its pencil chest, roughly traced over the construction lines, was a lantern: not real, but drawn in thick, unsteady ink, the way a child traces a picture through thin paper. On its shoulders were plates of glass: amber and green, traced in the same rough ink. And folded down its back, like a cloak, were letters. Envelopes. Postcards. Every god Wren had taken, the guardian was wearing a copy of. It was dressing itself in Wren's conquests.

It looked at Wren across the yard. Wren looked back.

*Look at us,* said the guardian. And it said it warmly. It had never said anything warmly before. *Look how strong we are now. Nobody will ever have to look after us again.*

Wren didn't say anything.

But they didn't disagree, either.

Then a mission had come, a small one, a farmer's god gone feral on the white road. And then another. And somehow the week after that there hadn't been time.

### Chapter 12: The Drowned Parish

The road to Saltby went down off the moor in a long switchback to the sea, and then it went into the sea.

Wren stood at the end of it. The road went on, quite clearly, under the water: a pale line of cobbles running down a slope of green and then on across the floor of what had been a valley, between the drowned walls of drowned fields, out toward a drowned village. Wren could see the roofs from here, under the water, slate after slate, green with weed. A church spire stuck up out of the middle of it all, the only thing above the surface. It had a boat tied to it.

It was raining. It was always raining, here. The water was very still, though, and very grey, and it had a weight to it. Even from the shore Wren could feel it. A heaviness in the air, as though the air itself had been soaked.

"Saltby," said the Warden who'd brought Wren down, a tall quiet man called Brother Anselm. "Six years ago, the sea wall went in a winter storm. The whole village. One survivor. A ferryman. Brennick." He pointed at the spire. "That's him."

"And the road?"

"The road was fine for five years. It went this spring. His god did it." Brother Anselm looked at the water with the mild, untroubled look all the Wardens had. "Three carts last winter. Two of the drivers drowned."

Wren went out to the spire in a borrowed dinghy. The rain came down on them the whole way, steady and soft, and the oars were heavy, and the water was heavier.

> ◆ **GAME** · Approach to the Drowned Parish. Rowing section, then exploration of the parish by boat and on the few surfaces above water. Optional: Watchlight reveals names chalked inside drowned houses, one per house. They're collectible, and they pay off in Chapter 23.

Hal Brennick was sitting in his boat inside the church.

The church was flooded to the gallery. The nave was a long still pool, with the tops of the pews just under the surface like the backs of sleeping animals, and the rain came in through the broken east window and fell on the water in rings. Hal's boat was tied to a pillar. He was sitting in it with his oars shipped, an old man in an oilskin, looking at nothing, and the rain ran off his hat.

His god was in the nave with him. Wren had never seen anything so big. It filled the church from the floor of the water to the roof-beams, a vast slow shape of dripping, sagging dark green, like seaweed hung out on a line in the rain, with long heavy arms that hung down into the water. It didn't move. It was crying. Water came off it everywhere, in streams and in sheets, into the nave, and the water in the nave rose.

"You're from the Order," said Hal, without turning round.

"Yes."

"Young."

"Yes."

"Hm." He looked at the god. "You've come for him."

"The road," Wren said. "The carts. People are —"

"I know." He nodded slowly. "I know about the carts. I'm sorry about the carts." He took off his hat and shook the rain out of it, and put it back on. "He's not killing me, you know. That's what you all think. He's not. He's just heavy." He looked up at the great sagging dripping shape that filled the church. "They were my whole life, that village. All of them. My Annie, my boys. The Mercers next door, and the school, and old Fenwick at the forge. I knew every soul. It's *supposed* to be heavy. What would it say about them, if it wasn't?"

The god's tears ran into the nave.

"I don't want to be Stilled," said Hal, quite simply. "I want to sit here. That's all. I'll move the road if you like. I'll talk to him. I'll try. But I don't want to be Stilled."

Wren sat in the dinghy with the oars across their knees.

"All right," said Wren.

Hal looked round at them, for the first time.

"All right?"

"I'll tell them it isn't feral. I'll tell them you'll move the road." Wren picked up the oars. "I'm sorry I came."

Wren turned the dinghy toward the broken east window, and the grey shore beyond it.

*Oh,* said the voice, *we're above this now, are we?*

Behind them, the water in the nave began to rise.

*We're the merciful one, now? You've done this three times. It gets easier. Let it get easier.*

Wren looked back. The guardian was standing on the surface of the nave, on the water, beside the vast weeping god, wearing its lantern and its glass and its letters, and it had laid one long pencil hand on the Undertow's dripping arm. And the Undertow had stopped crying. It had lifted its head.

The water was coming up the pillars. It was coming up the gallery stairs. Hal was on his feet in his boat, shouting, and the boat was rising with the water toward the roof-beams, and there was nowhere for the water to go. The broken east window was the only way out and the sea was coming in through it, not out, and the church was filling like a cup.

> ◆ **GAME** · Whichever choice the player made, the guardian whips the Undertow feral **the moment Wren turns to leave**. If the player tries to leave again, it happens again. Every time. **The player cannot win Hal's freedom. They can only choose to be the one who fights.** In Act 2, Wren's power always seems to arrive with the guardian's permission. That is the point.

> ◆ **BOSS: THE UNDERTOW** · A **grief** god. The arena (the flooded nave) gets **heavier the longer the fight goes on**: water rises in stages, movement slows, dodges recover more slowly, and the screen's edges darken and blur. There's no way to stop the rising. **Commit early**, or learn to fight well while dragged down. Its attacks are slow, huge and inevitable: long sweeping arms through the water, a crushing downward weight. Its weak points are on its back, reached with *Threshold* through the sweeps. The fight rewards accepting the weight rather than escaping it: the player's damage *increases* the lower they are in the water.

It was the hardest thing Wren had fought, and the slowest. It was like fighting the sea. Every blow Wren landed seemed to sink into it and go nowhere, and every blow it landed seemed to go on landing long after it had hit. The water rose. Wren fought on the pews, and then on the gallery, and then on the roof-beams, with the water lapping at their boots, and then at their knees. Every movement was harder. Every breath.

*Look at you,* said the guardian, from somewhere above the water, delighted. *Look how strong you are.*

When it was done, the Undertow was lying in the nave like a great green tarpaulin half-sunk in the water, and the water had stopped rising. Hal's boat had come to rest against the gallery rail. Hal was in it, holding the rail with both hands, looking at Wren.

**[ TAKE IT ]**

Wren took it. It went in heavy and slow, like cold water into a boot, and then it settled in the bottom of Wren somewhere, under everything else, and was still.

The water in the nave began to go down.

---

Hal Brennick rowed Wren back to the shore in the morning. He insisted. He had a good stroke for an old man, long and easy, and he took the boat out through the broken east window and across the drowned parish, over the drowned village, over the green slate roofs one after another.

He didn't look down once. He looked ahead, at the shore, with the rain running off his hat, and he smiled very slightly.

"Nice morning," he said.

Wren looked down. Under the water, a long way down, there was a school, with its bell in its little tower. There was a forge. There was a house with a blue door, the paint still on it, very faint, the only blue for a mile.

"Is it?" said Wren.

Hal considered the question, rowing. "I think so," he said. "I think it is."

### Chapter 13: Velle

Velle was the loudest place in the world. That was what everyone said, and it was true.

It was a city of theatres. There were forty of them, Wren was told on the way in, and nine opera houses and a hundred music halls and more cabarets than anyone had ever counted. The streets were narrow and steep and full of people, and every wall was covered in playbills, layers and layers of them, pasted over each other for a hundred years until the walls were soft with paper. It was night when they arrived, and it didn't matter; Velle was always night. Every street was lit with gaslight and limelight and the coloured glass of theatre lanterns, red and gold and violet, and the noise was enormous: barkers and orchestras and crowds and laughter, laughter everywhere, pouring out of every door.

It was the most vivid city Wren had ever seen. It made Corran look like a sketch. Wren walked through it in their white sash, and people moved aside for the sash. And some of them looked at it with fear.

The Order kept rooms in Velle, in a quiet white house on a quiet white square. They gave Wren the best room. Oren stayed in the next one. He liked Velle; he said it was very bright.

> ◆ **GAME** · **Velle hub.** A dense, vertical, noisy city at permanent night, the most saturated region in the game. NPCs react to Wren's **white sash**: some respectfully, many with fear, a few with open contempt (a street barker: *"Here comes the quiet. Hide your gods, ladies and gents!"*). Optional side errands around the theatres. In this city the guardian's taunts are drowned out by the noise, and it **sounds faintly nervous**.

The god that had put two stagehands in hospital belonged to Juniper Aske.

Everyone in Velle knew Juniper Aske. Her face was on every third playbill in the city. She was forty and had been the greatest actor in Velle for twenty years. She was playing, that season, at the Lyceum, the grandest theatre on the hill, in a tragedy that people wept at so much the management had started selling handkerchiefs in the foyer.

Wren went to see her before the show, in her dressing room, with the white sash on.

She was sitting at her mirror, putting on her face. She didn't turn round when Wren came in. She looked at Wren in the mirror, and went on painting one eyebrow, very precisely, in a long dark arch.

"Warden," she said. "How exciting. They've sent me a child."

"I'm twenty-four."

"Then they've sent me a child in a sash." She finished the eyebrow, and looked at it, critically, and began on the other. "You've come about the stagehands."

"Yes."

"I'm sorry about the stagehands. Tell them I'm sorry. Tell the Order I'm sorry." She put down the brush. "But I'm not coming to your quiet room."

Wren looked around the dressing room. It was small and crowded and bright, full of flowers and costumes and letters from admirers stuck around the mirror. And something else was in it. Something Wren couldn't see. Wren could feel it in the corner of their eye, the way you feel someone standing behind you in a queue. Every time Wren turned their head to look, there was nothing there. When Wren looked away, it was there again, just at the edge, tall and still.

"You can't see her," said Juniper. "Nobody can. That's rather the point." She smiled at herself in the mirror, a thin smile. "The Veiled. She only exists when no one's looking. Like all the best parts of us."

She began on her mouth, a dark red. She looked at Wren in the mirror while she did it, and her eyes were very sharp.

"You're doing a performance too," she said. "You know that?"

"I'm not."

"Darling, I'd know. It's my trade. You're doing 'the one who has it handled.'" She blotted her lips. "It's not bad. You've clearly been doing it for years. You hold your hands too still, though. That's the giveaway. People who actually have it handled fidget."

Wren looked down at their hands.

"I'm not performing," Wren said.

Juniper turned round on her stool, at last, and looked at Wren directly. She looked at the white sash, with its silver edge. And then back at Wren's face.

"Then what," she said kindly, "do you think the sash is?"

### Chapter 14: The Veiled

The play that night was *The Queen of Ashes*, and it was the best thing Wren had ever seen. Wren watched it from a box at the side of the stage, in the dark, with Oren beside them, and for two hours forgot everything.

Juniper was the Queen. She was magnificent. She was a different person; she was a dozen different people, all of them the Queen. She was furious and she was tender and she was terrible, and when in the second act her son betrayed her, she sat down on the floor of the stage like a child and said, *"Oh,"* very quietly, and two thousand people in the Lyceum made the same small sound at the same time.

And she was afraid. The whole time. Wren could see it from the box. Every time she came off into the wings, between scenes, she went to the same place, in the shadow behind the flats, and stood with her back to the stage and her hands pressed flat against her stomach, shaking, where nobody in the house could see. Then the cue came, and she straightened, and turned, and walked back out into the light as the Queen. And something was behind her the whole time. Something tall, that Wren couldn't quite see.

In the third act, there was a comic scene. A drunk porter, a gravedigger, an old Velle tradition, a bit of relief before the end. The audience laughed. The whole Lyceum laughed, two thousand people at once, a great warm roar of it rolling up out of the stalls.

In the wings, Juniper bent over. She didn't make a sound. She stood with her back to all that laughter and her shoulders going, crying the way people cry when they have learned to do it in a room with other people in it.

*Don't look at her,* said Wren's guardian.

It was in the box with them. Wren hadn't seen it come. It was standing at the back, in the dark, wearing all its traced ink, its lantern and glass and letters and weed, and it had never sounded like this before.

*Look away. Look at the stage. You know what happens when you look. You look, and then you can't stop knowing, and then you go and tell someone, and then —*

Wren didn't look away. Wren was looking at a woman crying with her back to the room.

And Wren felt something on the back of their own neck, cold, like a draught from a door left open fifteen years ago.

She came back at six. Wren was sitting on the stairs, on the fourth stair, where you could see.

She came through the door and took off her coat and hung it on the hook, the right way up, which she never did. That night there was no tap running. There was nothing through the wall at all.

Wren lay in bed in the dark and listened to the quiet, and the thing Wren felt was so big and so warm that it frightened them. It was a long time before they had a name for it. It was *relief*. On the pillow, the little brown bird stopped being a bird.

Wren didn't see it happen. Wren felt it. Something behind them, on the pillow, getting longer. Something that had been round and quick and small becoming tall and slow and grey. The fight runs through the Lyceum backstage *during the live performance*: the audience can be heard through the flats, and **each wave of audience laughter makes the Veiled stronger**. Phase 2 moves onto the stage itself, in front of the audience. The house lights are down, so the audience is only a dark mass, but it's watching.

It was everywhere and nowhere. It came out of the wings and hit Wren in the back and was gone. It came out of the flats. It came out of the dark between the hanging ropes in the fly-loft. Every time Wren turned to see it, there was nothing there. Every time Wren turned away, it was right behind them, tall, faceless, silent, with long hands.

*Don't look at it,* said Wren's guardian. It was everywhere too, now. In the ropes. In the painted flats. *Don't look. Don't let it see you looking.*

Wren stopped looking.

Wren stood in the dark of the wings and closed their eyes and *listened*, the way the Hound had listened for foxes. There, a footstep, behind the backdrop. There, a breath. There, in the long mirror by the quick-change booth, out of the corner of the eye: a shape. Wren turned *away* from it, and the Veiled was in the corner of their eye, solid, and Wren struck sideways without looking, and it cried out.

The laughter in the auditorium rolled up again, and the Veiled grew.

They fought through the wings, and up the fly-ladder, and along the catwalk over the stage, and down. And then they were on the stage. On the stage, in the light, in the middle of the third act of *The Queen of Ashes*, with two thousand people out in the dark. The porter and the gravedigger had fled. Juniper was in the wings with her hands over her face. The audience thought it was part of the play. They didn't know what they were watching. They laughed, in the wrong places, nervously. The Veiled grew enormous in the laughter, filling the proscenium arch.

*Not again,* the guardian was saying, *not again, not again,* and Wren didn't know any more whether it was talking to Wren or to itself.

At the end Wren stood in the middle of the stage, in the limelight, with their back to the Veiled, and their eyes on the dark auditorium, on the two thousand faces they couldn't see. And Wren waited. The Veiled came at their back. Wren turned, and struck, without looking, and the Veiled came down on the boards behind them.

The audience, uncertain, began to applaud.

**[ TAKE IT ]**

Wren took it. It went in like a held breath. Like something going round a corner just as you turn your head.

Then it was still.

> ◆ **GAME** · **UNWATCHED** acquired (from the Veiled). Combat: become unseen for a short time, and enemies lose track of Wren. Exploration: walk past sentries, and **see things that only exist when no one is looking** (hidden doors and objects that vanish under direct gaze). The guardian now wears a traced veil.

Juniper was Stilled.

She went on the next night. Wren was told about it at breakfast in the white house on the white square; the papers were full of it. The critics said it was the most technically perfect performance of her career. Every line exact. Every gesture placed. Not one moment out of true. In the second act, when her son betrayed her, she sat down on the stage and said *"Oh,"* very precisely, and it was perfect, and nobody in the audience made a sound.

She didn't read the reviews.

Wren saw her once more, before they left Velle, walking down the hill from the Lyceum in the late morning in a plain grey coat. She was walking slowly, looking in shop windows. She saw Wren across the street and nodded, pleasantly, as one nods to a familiar face whose name one can't quite place.

Wren nodded back.

"Who's that?" said Oren, beside them.

"An actor."

"Oh." He watched her go. "She looks nice."

*She's free,* said the guardian, softly. *Nobody will ever have to see her like that again.*

### Chapter 15: The Quiet Room

Wren came back to the Stillhouse with five gods inside them, and a gap in their outline that ran from the shoulder all the way down the outside of the arm to the wrist.

Nobody mentioned it. Wren hardly noticed it. It didn't hurt. Sometimes, when Wren moved quickly, the left arm felt a little light, a little thin, like paper in a draught. That was all.

Severine met them in the courtyard. She was smiling.

"We've found it," she said. "Come and see."

She took Wren up to the library in the north tower, where the Order kept its oldest books, and showed them an open folio on a lectern. It was very old, the ink brown. It showed a drawing of a figure kneeling, and around the figure a ring of Stillers, and above the figure, held in the air by lines, a god.

"The Order's oldest rite," said Severine. "From before the Fold. Before the hum. We stopped using it four hundred years ago. It needs something we don't have." She laid her finger on the drawing, on the lines that held the god in the air. "It needs the god held still. Not quieted. *Held*. Pinned, like a moth on a card, long enough for the Stillers to do their work. We could never do it. A god won't hold still for a stranger."

She looked at Wren.

"But you have five gods inside you, Wren. Five, that you've quieted with your own hand. Five, that will do as you ask." She closed the folio, very gently. "You could hold it."

Wren looked down at the closed book.

"Hold my own god down," Wren said, "while you erase it."

Severine didn't flinch from it. She never flinched from anything.

"Yes," she said.

*Do it,* said the guardian.

Wren looked up. It was in the library doorway, wearing every one of its traced, stolen pieces, and it was very tall now, as tall as the doorframe, and for the first time it was looking at Wren with something Wren had never once seen in it in fifteen years: fear.

*Do it. Go on. You're strong now. You're stronger than me. Isn't that what you wanted?*

It was telling Wren to do it. And it was afraid. Wren stood in the library and tried to understand how both of those things could be true, and couldn't.

"Tomorrow," said Wren.

---

The Quiet Room was at the heart of the Stillhouse, at the bottom of a long white stair. It was vast and white and round, perfectly round, and its floor was white stone and its walls were white stone and the dome above it was white, and it was so cleanly drawn that it hurt. There was no smudge in it anywhere. There was nothing in it at all.

The Stillers stood around the wall in a ring, twenty of them, hands folded. Severine stood at the head of the ring. Oren sat on a white bench by the door, with his hands on his knees, watching with polite interest, the way he watched everything.

Wren walked out alone into the middle of the floor.

"It will come," said Severine, across the white. "When it's threatened, it always comes. And when it does —" She smiled at Wren, and there was nothing in the smile but faith. "You'll know what to do. You're very good at this now."

It came.

It came up out of the white floor as though the floor were water, and it was enormous. It filled the Quiet Room to the dome. It was wearing everything: the Hound's lantern blazing in its chest, the Draft's glass armour over its shoulders and arms, the Courier's letters in a great flapping cloak down its back, the Undertow's dripping weed round its legs, and over its crosshair face, the Veiled's veil. It was a monster made of everything Wren had taken. And underneath all of it, under every traced, borrowed piece, the grey pencil construction lines were still there, thin and frightened, holding it all together.

> ◆ **BOSS: THE GUARDIAN (first fight)** · The biggest fight in the game so far, built to feel spectacular. The guardian uses **Wren's whole kit back against the player**: *Threshold* doors, *True Edge* counters, *Undertow* slams, *Unwatched* invisibility, *Watchlight* reads. It has been **learning all game**, and the director system's full habit profile is active at once. It fights the way **this player** fights. It rearranges the Quiet Room's geometry mid-fight: floors stretch into corridors, walls tilt, the dome comes down. **Its dialogue during the fight is a continuous rising monologue** (below). Three phases; the last is on a floor the guardian has tilted almost vertical.

*Everything you do,* said the guardian, *I've seen.*

It drew a door in the air and stepped through it and came out behind Wren. Wren's trick. Wren's own trick, done better.

*Every dodge. Every flinch. Every time you turned left. You think you're stronger now. You're louder. It isn't the same.*

It struck, and the strike was perfect, the Draft's glint on it. Wren barely got out from under it. It slammed down with the weight of the drowned parish and the white floor buckled.

*I was protecting you. I've always been protecting you. Do you know what they'd do, if they saw you as you are?*

It vanished. Wren couldn't see it, and couldn't look for it. Wren shut their eyes, and listened, and turned, and struck without looking, and hit something, and it cried out.

*Stop,* it said. *Stop it.*

It was coming apart. Its traced pieces were cracking off it, lantern and glass and letters, falling and shattering on the white floor like plates. Underneath, the grey pencil lines were showing through, more and more of them, fraying.

*Wren, stop, I'm trying to —*

Wren didn't stop.

At the end it was down on one knee in the middle of the white floor, with all its borrowed pieces in shards around it, and only the pencil left: the thin, grey, frightened underdrawing of something that had never been finished.

"*Now,*" said Severine. "Hold it now."

> ◆ **GAME** · **THE HOLDING.** The prompt is not "Take It". It's **[ HOLD IT ]**, and holding the button **calls each of Wren's five gods out in turn** to pin the guardian: the Hound's jaws on one arm, the Courier's paper binding the other, the Draft's glass encasing its legs, the Undertow's weight on its back, the Veiled across its face. The player has to **keep holding** while the Stillers' hum rises. It takes **twenty real seconds**. The guardian struggles at first, then stops struggling. It's the darkest image in the game, and it's the same thing Wren has done to other people's gods all act, now done to Wren's own.

Wren held it.

Wren called the gods out of their chest, one after another, and they came: and Wren set them on it. The Hound's jaws on its arm. The Courier's paper round its other arm, binding. The Draft's glass over its legs, setting hard. The Undertow's weight on its back, pressing it down to the white floor. And the Veiled, last of all, drawn over its face, so Wren wouldn't have to see it.

The Stillers began to hum. A low single note, twenty voices, rising.

The guardian struggled. It pulled against the Hound and the paper and the glass. Wren felt it, every pull, in their own arms and their own chest, like holding a door shut against someone on the other side. And Wren held.

The hum rose.

The guardian stopped struggling.

It lay quite still under the five gods, pinned to the white floor. And under the Veiled's veil, Wren heard it say something. Very quietly. Wren couldn't make out what. It might have been *please*. It might have been Wren's name.

Severine stepped forward into the ring. She knelt beside the pinned grey shape on the floor, and reached out two fingers, and laid them very gently on its chest.

And every line it was made of went out at once, from the inside, like a page being cleaned.

### Chapter 16: The Quiet

> ◆ **GAME** · **THE QUIET.** From the moment the guardian is erased: **all audio stops.** No music, no ambient sound, no footsteps, no UI sounds. Subtitles continue. The player can walk. That's all. **This section lasts 15–20 minutes of real play.** Colour drains from the screen slowly, a little each minute, until the world is fully greyscale and the player realizes they don't know when it happened. Wren speaks only when spoken to. Combat encounters still happen and are **easy**: no taunts, no adaptation, and every ability works perfectly every time. Resting shows a black frame and the word **MORNING**. There is no [ GET UP ] prompt. Wren is simply up.

Wren walked out of the Quiet Room.

Severine came across the white floor and embraced them, and held them for a long time, and Wren felt nothing about it, and that was fine. It was a perfectly pleasant thing to happen. The Stillers smiled. Wren smiled back. Oren got up off his bench by the door, and came over, and looked at Wren with mild, pleased interest, the way he'd looked at the bowl Ambrose made.

They went up the long white stair. At the top, the courtyard was full of sunlight. Wren noticed it was sunlight. That was all.

---

It was a very good week.

Wren slept. Wren had not slept a full night through, without waking, since the age of nine, and now Wren lay down at ten in the east tower room, and closed their eyes, and opened them and it was morning. There was no struggle in between. There was no lying on one's side in the grey, waiting to be the first part, and then the next part. There was no voice. There were no marks on the wall. Wren was simply up.

Wren ate. Wren noticed that food had a taste, and that the taste was there, and that was all there was to say about it. Wren trained in the yard in the mornings. Wren was flawless. The gods inside Wren did everything Wren asked without being asked twice, and the Wardens watched, and Wren noticed they were watching and did not mind.

There was a mission. A small one: a shepherd's god on the white road, gone feral after a bad winter, scattering the flock. Wren went. It was easy. The god came at Wren across the white hillside and Wren saw every blow it was going to make before it made it, and stepped through a door in the air, and struck once, and it was down. *Take it.* Wren took it. There was nobody laughing. There was nobody telling Wren anything at all. There was the white hillside and the quiet and the shepherd, smiling, thanking Wren very much.

Wren noticed, once, walking back along the white road, that they couldn't remember what colour the shepherd's god had been. Wren thought about it for a while. It didn't seem to matter.

---

On the seventh evening, Wren and Oren sat on the step of the east tower, by a lamp Oren had lit on the bracket there. It was a lovely evening. The white towers were pale gold in the last of the light. Wren knew they were gold. Wren could see that they were gold. It was simply true.

"You sleep now, too," said Oren.

"Yes."

He nodded, pleased. They sat. A long time went by.

"Is it good?" said Oren.

Wren thought about it. Wren gave the question their whole attention. It seemed important to get it right.

"I don't know," said Wren at last, "how I'd know."

Oren nodded again, as though that were a very reasonable answer.

That night Wren opened the letters.

There were fourteen. They'd been on the desk in the east tower room for weeks, in a stack, with the top one open where Wren had read the first line and folded it. Wren sat on the bed with the lamp lit and read them all, one after another, from the first to the last. And because Oren was there in the doorway, and it seemed polite, Wren read them out loud.

Wren's voice was level and pleasant and unhurried. It didn't catch on anything.

### Chapter 17: Underdrawing

On the ninth day, Severine sent Wren south.

"There's a god on the Tollway," she said, over breakfast, in the refectory. "A carter's. It's taken to blocking the road. Nothing serious. I thought you might like the walk. You came up that road, didn't you? You might like to see it again."

"All right," said Wren.

They went south, Wren and Oren, along the white road. Wren didn't follow the red thread. Wren didn't need to. They knew the way. The thread lay beside the road the whole time, a thin line of red on the white, running south, and Wren saw it, and noticed it was red, and walked on.

It was a pleasant walk. Five days on the white road. Then the hedges came back, a line at a time. Then the colour came back, a little, then a little more.

Except it didn't.

Wren noticed it on the seventh day, at Corran. It had been the most vivid place Wren had ever seen. Coming over the hill above the valley, Wren looked down at the glass roofs and the green spire and the furnaces, and it was grey. Not all of it. Not quite. The furnaces still glowed. But the glass was pale and the spire was pale and the whole town looked like a print of itself in a book, the colours put in by somebody in a hurry with a thin brush.

Wren looked at it for a while.

"It's paler," said Wren.

"Is it?" said Oren, interested. He looked at it. "I suppose it is."

They went on.

Three days south of Corran, there was a waystation on the moor road. It was a small one, a single house with a stable and a water-trough and a sign. A family kept it: a man, a woman, two children, and their gods. Wren had stopped there on the way north, months ago, and the woman had given them bread. Wren remembered that. Wren remembered that it had been good bread.

The waystation was still there.

But its floor was graph paper.

Wren stood in the doorway and looked at it. The floor of the house was a grid, faint blue lines on white, and the table stood on it, and the table was drawn, but only just. Its edges were a single line, no thickness to them. The walls were construction lines. Perspective lines ran from every corner to a vanishing point somewhere behind the stove. The family were at the table, having their supper, and they were drawn in pencil. Light grey pencil, the kind you put down first before you ink. You could see through them. You could see the grid of the floor through the woman's hands as she cut the bread.

They looked up and smiled at Wren.

"Evening," said the woman, in a voice like a voice heard through a wall. "You'll be wanting a bed."

Behind Wren, on the moor road, the sky was beginning to go to grid. Faint squares were drawing themselves across it, from the horizon up.

And the sound came back.

> ◆ **GAME** · **Audio returns all at once**, as a single overwhelming sound: **a scratching pencil**, loud, everywhere, from every direction. Colour does *not* return. From this point the world around Wren **turns to underdrawing as Wren moves through it**: construction-line shaders spread out from Wren in a radius that grows over the rest of the act. NPCs within the radius become pencil sketches. This is the guardian, **in the paper**.

It came all at once. Every sound in the world, back, in a single rush. And the loudest of them was a sound Wren knew better than their own heartbeat: the scratch of a pencil on paper. Close. Everywhere. In the walls. In the sky. In the grid on the floor. Scratching and scratching. Lines being drawn, and lines being rubbed out, and lines being drawn again.

And with it, feeling. All of it, at once, like a limb waking up after being sat on. Wren went down on one knee in the doorway of the waystation and put a hand over their mouth.

The guardian hadn't been erased.

Wren understood it, kneeling there in the doorway with the family smiling at them through their own pencil faces. Stilling erases a god's lines: that was what Severine had done, in the Quiet Room, with her two fingers. She had taken out every line it was made of. But the guardian wasn't *made* of lines, the way the Hound was made of orange ink or the Draft of glass. The guardian *was* lines. It was the underdrawing. The first marks under everything Wren had ever been. And when Severine took away its body, it hadn't gone. It had gone *down*. Into the paper. Under everything. And now it was spreading out from Wren, through the world, the way damp spreads through a wall.

Wren wasn't being followed by it any more.

Wren was carrying it, like weather.

And it wasn't speaking. That was the worst of it. In fifteen years it had never once been silent for more than a day. Now there was the scratching, everywhere, and under the scratching, nothing. It had nothing to say. It was just there, in the paper of the world, around Wren, wherever Wren went, rubbing things out.

"Wren?" said Oren, behind them, mildly. "Are you all right?"

Wren looked down at their own left arm.

The gap in the outline ran from the shoulder down the whole length of the arm, and down the side, past the ribs, to the hip. It had opened while Wren wasn't looking. Through the gap, where Wren should have been, there was nothing at all. Not skin. Not ink. Just paper, clean and white.

Wren sat in the doorway of the waystation for a long time, with the pencil family behind them eating their pencil supper. The sky over the moor went on turning to grid.

Then Wren got up, and turned round, and started walking north again, back to the Stillhouse. Fast.

Oren followed. He didn't ask why.
