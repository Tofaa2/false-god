# FALSE GOD — Story Treatments

Four complete story treatments for the same core premise. Each one takes the premise toward a different ending and a different argument. They are written to be read by someone who has never heard the pitch: a publisher, a critic, a collaborator.

| # | Title | Protagonist | Ending | What it argues |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | [The Unfinished](01-the-unfinished.md) | Wren, 24, dye-works labourer | **Tragedy.** Wren does everything right, and the guardian wins anyway. Mira, who loves them, finds them in the morning. | The voice is wrong about everything, and it wins. Being wrong doesn't stop it. |
| 2 | [Clean Lines](02-clean-lines.md) | Maren Sollis, 29, the Order's best god-hunter | **Pyrrhic victory.** Maren kills her guardian and becomes a saint of an empty world. (Hidden alternate ending.) | Silencing the voice completely is a death that looks like success. |
| 3 | [Weather](03-weather.md) | Ilse Harrow, 41, Crown surveyor | **Coexistence.** Ilse lives, the guardian stays, and nothing is cured. | You don't defeat your mind. You learn to live in it, with other people. |
| 4 | [Two Voices](04-two-voices.md) | Sef Adair, 26, pianist, **and his guardian** (both playable) | **Branching.** Four endings, decided by both halves of the player. | The player is the critic too. |

> **The chosen story, The Unfinished, has been revised and written as a full novel** in [`novel/`](novel/00-revision-notes.md): 30 chapters, about 43,000 words, with gameplay blocks marking how each chapter maps to play. **Where the novel and the treatment disagree, the novel is canonical.** The treatment still describes the earlier, redemptive ending. Start with the revision notes.

## Shared foundation

All four stories use this world. Individual treatments change details where the story needs it.

### The world is drawn

The visual style (stylized, toon-shaded, inked, animated in stepped poses like a hand-drawn film) is **literally true inside the fiction**. The world is a drawing, and something is still drawing it.

People believe the world is drawn by **the arguments between people and their gods**. Every living person's friction with their own mind adds line and colour to the world around them. Towns where people are loud, conflicted and alive are vivid and thickly inked.

### The Fade

For three generations the world has been fading. Colour drains first, then line weight, then detail, until an area becomes **an unfinished sketch**: construction lines, smudges, blank paper. Things inside the Fade still exist, but thinly. People there move through their lives correctly and without effort.

Most people believe the Fade is a natural decline, like an ageing sun.

### Gods

Everyone is born with a god: a guardian manifestation of their own mind, visible to everyone. A newborn's god is small, a scribble the size of a cat. Gods grow and change shape as their hosts do. They are not separate beings, though the culture has spent centuries pretending they are. People talk to their gods, argue with them, are embarrassed by them, love them.

Most gods are mostly protective and occasionally unbearable. A healthy relationship with your god looks like a good long marriage: constant bickering, and real devotion underneath.

When a person is in crisis, their god can go **feral**. It grows, distorts and turns violent toward its host, toward others, toward the world.

### The Order of the Still Hand

A respected, centuries-old institution that offers **Stilling**: the permanent quieting of a god. A Stilled person (the **Stilled**) is calm, functional, pleasant and permanently flat. They sleep well. They stop crying. They stop wanting much of anything.

The Order is sincere. Its members believe suffering is the one true evil and that Stilling is mercy. Their seat is **the Stillhouse**, a white citadel in the north whose linework is so perfect it looks unreal.

What the Order does not say, and in some stories does not know: **every Stilling feeds the Fade.** A world without arguments has nothing drawing it.

### The premise every story shares

Each protagonist's guardian has turned on them. In each story the guardian is a version of the same thing, the internal voice that says you are inadequate, but each story asks a different question about it:

- **The Unfinished:** what if you do everything right and it still wins?
- **Clean Lines:** what if you kill it and win?
- **Weather:** what if it never really leaves?
- **Two Voices:** what if you have to be it?

## Shared rules for writing this material

These rules apply across all four stories. The game deals with depression, shame, self-hatred and, implicitly, suicidal thinking. A guardian that tries to kill its host is a metaphor for suicidal ideation, and players who have lived with it will read it that way.

1. **The voice is never proven right.** It can win. It is never right. Even in the tragedies, the story shows that the guardian's claims, *trying is pointless* and *nobody will notice*, are false.
2. **Death is never presented as release.** When characters die, it is a loss. Players should feel it as one.
3. **No method detail, ever,** literal or metaphorical.
4. **The Stilled are not villains and not jokes.** They are people who chose relief, and the cost of that choice should be shown with compassion.
5. **No cures.** Characters who get better still struggle. Characters who make peace still argue.
6. **A content note at launch and a resources link in the menu** (localized helplines). Brief and non-preachy.
7. **Before shipping, the script gets read by people with lived experience.**

## Which one to build?

My recommendation is **The Unfinished** as the main game. It has the strongest single emotional arc, it uses every mechanic from the original pitch, and its ending uses the core system against the player: the final boss is a win that doesn't save Wren, and the last chapter takes away the **[ GET UP ]** prompt the player has held every morning since the first scene.

**Two Voices** is the most original and the most likely to get critics talking, but playing as the guardian is effectively a second game, and it's out of scope for a solo developer unless that's the *only* idea built.

**Clean Lines** and **Weather** both work well as smaller games, or as material to pull from. Weather's hub and cooperative abilities and Clean Lines' final parry prompt are both worth borrowing.
